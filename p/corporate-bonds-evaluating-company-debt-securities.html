Corporate Bonds: Evaluating Company Debt Securities

Corporate bonds allow investors to lend money to companies in exchange for interest payments and the eventual repayment of principal. They can provide income and diversification within a broader investment portfolio, but the return offered by a corporate bond is closely connected to the financial condition of the company issuing the debt.

Unlike stocks, corporate bonds do not represent an ownership interest in the company. Bondholders are creditors. The company has contractual obligations under the bond's terms, including scheduled interest payments and repayment of principal at maturity.

That distinction can make corporate bonds useful for investors seeking fixed-income exposure, but it also introduces several risks. A company can experience financial difficulties, interest rates can change, and a bond's market price can fluctuate before maturity.

Evaluating a corporate bond therefore requires looking beyond its coupon rate or advertised yield.

How Corporate Bonds Work

When a company issues a bond, it borrows money from investors.

The company agrees to pay interest according to the bond's terms and repay the principal at maturity, assuming it remains financially capable of doing so and no other event changes the obligation.

For example, a company might issue a $1,000 bond with a 5% annual coupon and a five-year maturity. An investor holding the bond under its original terms could receive $50 per year in interest and the $1,000 principal at maturity.

Corporate bonds can be issued by large publicly traded companies, private companies, financial institutions, utilities, and other businesses.

They can vary significantly in maturity, credit quality, interest rate, seniority, collateral, call provisions, and liquidity.

Corporate Bonds vs. Stocks

Stocks and corporate bonds both provide exposure to a company, but they represent fundamentally different claims.

A stock represents ownership. Shareholders may benefit from increases in the company's value and, in some cases, receive dividends.

A corporate bond represents debt. The bondholder generally has a contractual claim for interest and principal according to the bond agreement.

If a company encounters financial distress, bondholders generally have a higher claim on company assets than common shareholders, although the actual recovery depends on the company's capital structure, the bond's seniority, collateral, bankruptcy proceedings, and other factors.

This does not make corporate bonds risk-free. It simply means the investor occupies a different position in the company's financial structure.

Coupon Rate and Yield

The coupon rate is the interest rate specified by the bond's terms.

Yield reflects the return available based on the bond's current market price and expected cash flows.

A corporate bond issued at $1,000 with a 6% coupon would generally pay $60 per year in interest. If the bond later trades for $950, its current yield based on the coupon and market price would be higher than 6%.

If it trades for $1,050, the current yield would be lower.

Investors should therefore examine the bond's yield rather than relying exclusively on its coupon rate.

Yield to maturity can provide a broader measure by incorporating the purchase price, coupon payments, and principal repayment, assuming the bond is held to maturity and the issuer fulfills its obligations.

For callable bonds, yield to call can also be relevant.

Credit Risk Is Central to Corporate Bonds

The primary risk specific to corporate debt is credit risk.

Credit risk is the possibility that the issuer may be unable to make scheduled interest or principal payments.

A company's ability to repay its debt depends on factors such as revenue, profitability, cash flow, debt levels, refinancing needs, liquidity, industry conditions, and access to capital markets.

A company with strong and stable cash flows may generally have greater capacity to service debt than a highly leveraged business with unpredictable revenue.

Investors should therefore examine the company's financial condition rather than treating the stated yield as a standalone measure of value.

Investment-Grade and High-Yield Bonds

Corporate bonds are often grouped according to credit quality.

Investment-grade bonds are issued by companies with relatively strong credit profiles according to major rating agencies.

High-yield bonds, sometimes called speculative-grade bonds, have lower credit ratings and generally offer higher yields to compensate investors for taking greater credit risk.

The higher yield is not simply additional income without additional risk.

A company with weaker credit quality may face greater probability of default, greater price volatility, and more difficulty refinancing its obligations.

Investors considering high-yield corporate bonds should therefore examine the reason for the higher yield and the financial risks associated with the issuer.

Understanding Credit Ratings

Credit-rating agencies such as Moody's, S&P Global Ratings, and Fitch assess the credit quality of many corporate issuers and their debt securities.

Ratings can help investors compare the relative credit quality of different bonds.

However, a credit rating is an opinion rather than a guarantee.

Ratings can also change. A company experiencing deteriorating financial conditions may receive a downgrade, potentially causing its bond prices to fall and borrowing costs to rise.

Investors should therefore treat ratings as one research input rather than a substitute for evaluating the company's financial statements and debt structure.

Bond Seniority Matters

Not all corporate debt has the same position in a company's capital structure.

Senior debt generally has a higher claim on assets and cash flows than subordinated debt.

If a company enters bankruptcy or restructuring, the priority of different claims can affect how much investors ultimately recover.

A senior secured bond may have claims supported by specific collateral, while an unsecured bond relies more broadly on the issuer's creditworthiness.

Subordinated or junior debt generally sits lower in the repayment hierarchy.

Two bonds issued by the same company can therefore have different risk characteristics even if they have similar maturities and coupon rates.

Secured vs. Unsecured Corporate Bonds

A secured corporate bond is backed by specified collateral.

If the issuer defaults, the collateral may provide an additional source of recovery, subject to the terms of the bond and applicable legal proceedings.

Unsecured bonds do not have specific collateral pledged to support them.

Instead, repayment depends on the issuer's general creditworthiness and the bond's position within the company's capital structure.

Investors should review the offering documents to understand whether a bond is secured, what assets serve as collateral, and what claims other creditors may have.

Maturity and Interest-Rate Risk

Corporate bonds are affected by changes in market interest rates.

When rates rise, existing fixed-rate bonds generally become less attractive compared with newly issued securities. Their market prices can decline.

When rates fall, existing bonds with relatively higher coupons can become more valuable.

Longer-maturity corporate bonds generally have greater sensitivity to interest-rate changes than shorter-maturity bonds.

Duration can help investors estimate this sensitivity.

An investor holding an individual corporate bond until maturity may be less concerned about interim price fluctuations, assuming the issuer remains able to repay the debt.

Someone who may need to sell before maturity must consider the possibility of receiving less than the original purchase price.

Callable Corporate Bonds

Some companies issue bonds that can be redeemed before maturity.

These callable bonds give the issuer the right to repay the debt under specified conditions.

Companies may choose to call bonds when interest rates decline and refinancing becomes cheaper.

For investors, this can create reinvestment risk. A bond offering an attractive interest rate may be redeemed when comparable new investments offer lower yields.

When evaluating callable corporate debt, investors should review the call dates, call price, yield-to-call, and yield-to-maturity.

The highest advertised yield may not represent the return the investor ultimately receives.

Liquidity and the Corporate Bond Market

Corporate bonds vary considerably in liquidity.

Some bonds issued by large companies trade relatively frequently, while other securities may have limited secondary-market activity.

Liquidity matters because investors who need to sell quickly may receive a price below the value they expected.

Bid-ask spreads can also increase transaction costs.

Before purchasing an individual corporate bond, investors should consider how actively the security trades and whether there is a practical secondary market.

Bond ETFs and mutual funds can provide a different approach by holding diversified portfolios of corporate debt rather than requiring investors to trade individual securities.

Corporate Bond Funds and ETFs

Corporate bond funds can provide exposure to many issuers through a single investment.

A fund may hold dozens, hundreds, or potentially thousands of bonds, depending on its strategy.

This can reduce the impact of one company experiencing financial difficulties, although it does not eliminate credit risk.

Investors should review a corporate bond fund's:

  • Average and effective duration
  • Credit-quality distribution
  • Investment-grade or high-yield exposure
  • Expense ratio
  • Average maturity
  • Sector concentration
  • Geographic exposure
  • Portfolio holdings
  • Yield methodology

A bond fund also behaves differently from an individual bond. It generally has no single maturity date and can continue buying and selling securities as part of its strategy.

Comparing Corporate Bond Yields

A higher yield can be attractive, but it should always be viewed alongside risk.

A company may offer a higher yield because investors perceive greater credit risk. Alternatively, the yield may reflect longer maturity, lower liquidity, or other structural characteristics.

Investors can compare a corporate bond's yield with Treasury securities of similar maturity to examine its credit spread.

The difference between a corporate bond yield and a comparable Treasury yield is commonly referred to as the credit spread.

A wider spread generally indicates that investors are demanding more compensation for the additional risks associated with corporate debt.

Spreads can change over time as market perceptions of credit risk change.

Corporate Bond Tax Considerations

Interest from most corporate bonds is generally subject to federal income tax and, depending on the investor's circumstances, state and local taxes.

This differs from the treatment that can apply to certain municipal bond interest.

For taxable investors, the after-tax yield can therefore be more relevant than the stated yield.

Corporate bonds held inside tax-advantaged retirement accounts are subject to the rules governing those accounts rather than being taxed annually in the same way as interest received in a taxable brokerage account.

Investors should consider tax treatment alongside yield, maturity, credit quality, and account location.

Inflation and Reinvestment Risk

Corporate bond investors also face inflation risk.

If inflation rises substantially, the purchasing power of fixed interest payments can decline.

Reinvestment risk occurs when an investor receives interest or principal and must reinvest it at lower prevailing rates.

These risks can become particularly relevant for investors relying on bond income to fund future expenses.

A diversified maturity structure or bond ladder can help spread reinvestment dates, although it cannot eliminate the underlying risk.

How to Evaluate a Corporate Bond

Before purchasing an individual corporate bond, investors can examine several factors:

  1. Issuer: Who is borrowing the money?
  2. Credit quality: What are the issuer's ratings and financial characteristics?
  3. Maturity: When is principal scheduled to be repaid?
  4. Yield: What return is available at the current purchase price?
  5. Duration: How sensitive is the bond to interest-rate changes?
  6. Seniority: Where does the bond rank among the company's creditors?
  7. Collateral: Is the debt secured?
  8. Call provisions: Can the issuer repay the bond early?
  9. Liquidity: How actively does the bond trade?
  10. Taxes: What will the investor retain after applicable taxes?
  11. Fees: What are the transaction costs and bid-ask spread?

This process helps investors evaluate the entire security rather than focusing on one attractive feature.

Where Corporate Bonds Fit in a Portfolio

Corporate bonds can provide income and diversify exposure to equities and government securities.

An investor might combine Treasury securities, municipal bonds, corporate bonds, and other fixed-income investments to create different levels of credit and interest-rate exposure.

The appropriate allocation depends on the investor's objectives, time horizon, risk tolerance, tax position, and existing assets.

Investors seeking lower credit risk may emphasize higher-quality corporate debt, while those willing to accept greater credit risk may consider high-yield securities.

Bond funds can simplify diversification, while individual bonds can provide specific maturity dates and contractual cash flows.

Neither approach eliminates risk.

Corporate bonds can be useful investments, but the interest payment is only one part of the analysis. The issuer's ability to repay, the bond's position in the capital structure, maturity, yield, liquidity, call provisions, taxes, and prevailing market conditions all influence the potential outcome.

Understanding these factors allows investors to evaluate corporate debt more carefully and determine how it fits alongside stocks, Treasury securities, municipal bonds, and other investments within a broader portfolio.
