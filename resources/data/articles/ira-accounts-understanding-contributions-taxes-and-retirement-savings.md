When building a long-term wealth strategy, a taxable brokerage account provides substantial flexibility, but it does not offer the same tax treatment as a retirement account. **Individual Retirement Accounts (IRAs)** provide tax-advantaged structures that can help individuals accumulate and invest money for retirement.

Traditional and Roth IRAs use different tax models. Understanding **IRA contribution limits, Roth IRA income limits, Traditional IRA deductions, tax-deferred growth, qualified withdrawals, required minimum distributions, and early withdrawal rules** can help investors determine how these accounts fit into a broader retirement strategy.

## Traditional vs. Roth IRAs

Both Traditional and Roth IRAs are individual retirement accounts, but they treat contributions and withdrawals differently.

### Traditional IRA

Traditional IRA contributions may be deductible depending on the investor's income, filing status, and whether the investor or spouse is covered by an employer-sponsored retirement plan.

Money inside a Traditional IRA generally grows tax-deferred. Investment earnings and gains generally are not taxed while they remain in the account.

When taxable distributions are taken, they are generally included in ordinary income. If an investor has made nondeductible contributions, the tax treatment can be more complicated because part of a distribution may represent previously taxed basis.

Traditional IRAs are also generally subject to **required minimum distributions (RMDs)** beginning at age 73 under current federal rules.

### Roth IRA

Roth IRA contributions are made with after-tax dollars and are not deductible.

The primary tax benefit comes later: **qualified Roth IRA distributions can be tax-free**, including qualifying investment earnings. A qualified distribution generally requires satisfying applicable conditions, including the Roth five-year rule and an applicable qualifying event such as reaching age 59½.

Roth IRAs also do not require distributions during the original owner's lifetime.

An important distinction is that Roth IRA contributions are subject to ordering rules. Regular contributions are generally treated as distributed before conversion or rollover amounts and earnings, meaning a withdrawal of regular contributions can generally be tax-free. This does not mean that all Roth IRA withdrawals are automatically tax-free.

## 2026 IRA Contribution Limits

The IRS limits the amount an individual can contribute to Traditional and Roth IRAs each year.

For **2026**, total contributions across all of an individual's Traditional and Roth IRAs cannot exceed:

* **\$7,500** for individuals under age 50
* **\$8,600** for individuals age 50 or older, including the \$1,100 catch-up contribution

The combined limit applies across Traditional and Roth IRAs rather than providing a separate \$7,500 limit for each account.

Contributions also cannot exceed the individual's taxable compensation for the year.

For example, an investor could contribute \$4,000 to a Roth IRA and \$3,500 to a Traditional IRA in 2026, assuming the investor otherwise qualifies and has sufficient taxable compensation.

## Roth IRA Income Eligibility

Unlike Traditional IRA contributions, direct Roth IRA contributions are subject to income limits.

The applicable limit is based on **modified adjusted gross income (MAGI)** and filing status. Investors whose income falls within the applicable phase-out range may be able to make a reduced contribution, while investors above the applicable threshold may not be eligible for a direct Roth IRA contribution.

Because IRS thresholds can change annually, investors should verify the current-year limits before making a contribution.

High-income investors may also encounter strategies involving Roth conversions, including so-called **backdoor Roth IRA contributions**. These transactions can have tax consequences, particularly when an investor already holds pre-tax Traditional, SEP, or SIMPLE IRA assets, so the applicable rules should be reviewed carefully.

## Traditional IRA Deduction Rules

A Traditional IRA does not automatically provide a tax deduction simply because an investor contributes money.

Whether a contribution is deductible depends on several factors, including:

* Filing status
* Modified adjusted gross income
* Whether the investor is covered by a workplace retirement plan
* Whether the investor's spouse is covered by a workplace retirement plan

For 2026, the IRS lists different deduction phase-out thresholds depending on filing status and workplace retirement-plan coverage.

Investors making nondeductible Traditional IRA contributions should maintain accurate tax records because those contributions create basis that can affect the taxation of future distributions.

## IRA Investment Options

An IRA is an account structure rather than a specific investment.

Depending on the financial institution, an IRA may provide access to investments such as:

* Individual stocks
* ETFs
* Mutual funds
* Bonds
* Treasury securities
* Certificates of deposit
* Money market investments

The available investment menu depends on the brokerage, bank, or other financial institution where the IRA is established.

Investors should compare **IRA investment options, expense ratios, trading costs, account fees, minimum investment requirements, and available retirement-planning tools** before opening an account.

## Early IRA Withdrawals

IRAs are designed primarily for retirement savings, so withdrawals before age 59½ can trigger additional taxes.

### Traditional IRA Withdrawals

A taxable Traditional IRA distribution taken before age 59½ generally may be subject to ordinary income tax plus a **10% additional tax**, unless an exception applies.

The IRS provides several exceptions to the additional 10% tax, including certain distributions for qualified higher-education expenses, qualified first-time homebuyer expenses subject to a lifetime limit, and certain unreimbursed medical expenses.

Meeting an exception to the 10% additional tax does not necessarily make the underlying distribution income-tax-free.

### Roth IRA Withdrawals

Roth IRA withdrawals require different analysis.

Regular Roth contributions are generally distributed first under the IRS ordering rules. Because those contributions were already taxed, their return generally is not included in taxable income.

Conversions, rollovers, and investment earnings are subject to additional ordering and qualification rules. Earnings distributed through a nonqualified withdrawal may be taxable and may also be subject to the 10% additional tax unless an exception applies.

This distinction is important when evaluating a Roth IRA as part of an emergency-access or early-retirement strategy.

## Required Minimum Distributions

Traditional IRAs generally require owners to begin taking RMDs at age 73 under current federal law.

The first RMD generally must be taken by April 1 of the year following the year the owner reaches age 73, with later RMDs generally due by December 31 each year.

Roth IRAs do not require RMDs during the original owner's lifetime.

RMD rules can affect retirement income planning, taxable income, Medicare-related tax considerations, charitable giving strategies, and the amount of money remaining in retirement accounts.

## How to Use an IRA in a Retirement Strategy

A practical IRA strategy can involve several steps.

### 1\. Determine Your Eligibility

Review your taxable compensation, filing status, income, and access to employer-sponsored retirement plans.

This helps determine whether you can contribute to a Roth IRA and whether Traditional IRA contributions may be deductible.

### 2\. Select the Appropriate IRA Type

Compare the potential value of a current-year Traditional IRA deduction with the potential value of tax-free qualified Roth IRA distributions.

Your current and expected future tax circumstances can be relevant to this decision.

### 3\. Automate Contributions

The 2026 contribution limit of \$7,500 for investors under 50 works out to **\$625 per month** when divided across 12 months.

Automated monthly contributions can make it easier to maintain a consistent retirement savings schedule.

### 4\. Choose Investments

An IRA can hold a diversified portfolio of stocks, bonds, ETFs, mutual funds, or other eligible investments.

Consider **asset allocation, diversification, investment fees, time horizon, and risk tolerance** rather than selecting investments solely because they have recently performed well.

### 5\. Coordinate Multiple Accounts

Investors who have both taxable brokerage accounts and retirement accounts can consider how assets are distributed between them.

This process, known as **asset location**, may involve placing certain tax-inefficient investments in tax-advantaged accounts while holding more tax-efficient investments in taxable accounts.

However, tax efficiency should be considered alongside investment availability, diversification, liquidity, and the overall portfolio.

## IRA Accounts and Long-Term Wealth Building

Traditional and Roth IRAs can provide valuable tax advantages, but they are not interchangeable.

Traditional IRAs generally emphasize **tax-deferred contributions and withdrawals**, subject to deduction eligibility and distribution rules. Roth IRAs emphasize **after-tax contributions and potentially tax-free qualified distributions**.

The appropriate structure depends on factors such as income, tax bracket, retirement horizon, employer retirement benefits, expected future income, and the investor's broader financial plan.

For long-term investors, the combination of **consistent contributions, diversified investments, controlled fees, tax-efficient account selection, and disciplined portfolio management** can make an IRA an important component of retirement planning.
