For many employees, a **401(k) plan** is one of the primary vehicles for building retirement savings through automatic payroll contributions, tax-advantaged investment growth, and potential employer contributions. Workplace retirement plans can also provide access to diversified investments such as mutual funds, target-date funds, stock funds, and bond funds.

Understanding the difference between **Traditional and Roth 401(k) contributions, employer matching, vesting schedules, contribution limits, investment fees, and withdrawal rules** can help employees make more informed retirement-planning decisions.

## Traditional vs. Roth 401(k) Contributions

Many employer-sponsored plans offer both Traditional and Roth contribution options, although availability depends on the specific plan.

### Traditional 401(k)

Traditional 401(k) contributions are generally made on a pre-tax basis for federal income-tax purposes. The contribution can reduce current federal taxable income, while the money generally grows tax-deferred inside the account.

For example, an employee earning \$100,000 who contributes \$15,000 to a Traditional 401(k) generally has \$15,000 less in wages subject to current federal income tax, subject to applicable rules.

The investment earnings remain inside the account without current federal income taxation. When taxable distributions are eventually taken, they are generally included in ordinary income.

Traditional 401(k) contributions can therefore provide a current tax benefit in exchange for taxation when money is distributed.

### Roth 401(k)

Roth 401(k) contributions are made with after-tax dollars and generally do not provide a current federal income-tax deduction.

The potential benefit comes later. **Qualified Roth 401(k) distributions can generally be tax-free**, including investment earnings, when applicable requirements are satisfied.

Roth 401(k) contributions can therefore provide tax diversification for employees who expect their future tax circumstances to differ from their current situation.

It is important to distinguish qualified Roth distributions from simply withdrawing money from a Roth 401(k). Tax-free treatment depends on applicable qualification requirements.

## 2026 401(k) Contribution Limits

The IRS sets annual limits on employee elective deferrals and total contributions to defined contribution retirement plans.

For **2026**, the employee elective deferral limit for most 401(k) plans is:

**\$24,500**

This limit applies collectively to an employee's elective deferrals across applicable plans, rather than providing a separate \$24,500 limit for every employer plan. ([IRS](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/plan-participant-employee/retirement-topics-401k-and-profit-sharing-plan-contribution-limits))

### Catch-Up Contributions

Employees who are age 50 or older by the end of the calendar year may be eligible to make additional catch-up contributions if their plan permits them.

For 2026, the standard 401(k) catch-up contribution limit is:

**\$8,000**

Employees who **attain age 60, 61, 62, or 63 during 2026** may be eligible for the higher SECURE 2.0 catch-up limit of:

**\$11,250**

These additional contributions are separate from the regular \$24,500 elective deferral limit. ([IRS](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/plan-participant-employee/retirement-topics-catch-up-contributions))

Beginning in 2026, certain higher-income participants who make catch-up contributions may also be required to make those catch-up contributions as Roth contributions, depending on their prior-year wages and the applicable plan rules. ([IRS](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/plan-participant-employee/retirement-topics-catch-up-contributions))

### Total Annual Contributions

The 2026 defined contribution plan annual-additions limit is generally **\$72,000**, excluding catch-up contributions.

This limit can include:

* Employee elective deferrals
* Employer matching contributions
* Employer nonelective contributions
* Certain profit-sharing contributions

The applicable limit can also be constrained by compensation rules. ([IRS](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/plan-participant-employee/retirement-topics-401k-and-profit-sharing-plan-contribution-limits))

## Employer Matching Contributions

One of the major features of many workplace retirement plans is an **employer matching contribution**.

For example, an employer might contribute 50 cents for every dollar an employee contributes, up to a specified percentage of compensation.

The exact formula varies by employer.

An employer match can increase the amount invested for retirement without requiring the employee to contribute the entire amount personally. However, employees should review the plan's matching formula, eligibility requirements, and vesting schedule.

### Understanding Vesting

An employee's own 401(k) contributions are generally immediately vested.

Employer contributions may follow a **vesting schedule**, meaning an employee may need to remain with the company for a certain period before gaining full ownership of some employer contributions.

Plans can use different vesting structures, so employees should review the Summary Plan Description and plan documents.

## 401(k) Investment Options

A 401(k) is an account structure rather than a single investment.

The plan determines which investments employees can choose from. Common options include:

* U.S. stock mutual funds
* International stock funds
* Bond funds
* Target-date funds
* Stable-value investments
* Money market investments
* Company stock, where offered

The investment menu can vary considerably between employers.

### Target-Date Funds

Target-date funds are designed around an anticipated retirement year.

The fund's asset allocation generally changes over time, typically becoming more conservative as the target date approaches.

These funds can simplify portfolio management, but employees should review the **expense ratio, underlying holdings, asset allocation, and glide path** before selecting one.

### Index Funds

Some 401(k) plans offer low-cost index funds designed to track market benchmarks.

When comparing investment options, employees should examine expense ratios and other plan-level investment costs because even relatively small recurring fees can affect long-term retirement savings.

## 401(k) Fees

Investment performance is not the only factor affecting retirement account growth.

A 401(k) may involve several types of costs, including:

* Investment expense ratios
* Administrative fees
* Recordkeeping fees
* Individual service fees
* Advisory or managed-account fees, where applicable

Employees should review the plan's fee disclosures and compare the costs of available investments.

A lower-cost fund is not automatically appropriate for every portfolio, but fees are an important part of evaluating long-term retirement investments.

## Traditional 401(k) vs. Roth 401(k)

The two contribution structures can be summarized as follows:

| Feature | Traditional 401(k) | Roth 401(k) |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| Contributions | Generally pre-tax | After-tax |
| Current federal income-tax deduction | Generally yes | Generally no |
| Investment growth | Tax-deferred | Generally tax-free within account |
| Qualified withdrawals | Generally taxable | Generally tax-free |
| Income limit for employee contributions | No Roth-style income phase-out | No Roth IRA-style income phase-out |
| Employer availability | Depends on plan | Depends on plan |

The choice does not necessarily have to be all-or-nothing. If a plan permits both options, an employee may divide contributions between Traditional and Roth 401(k) accounts.

## 401(k) Withdrawals and Rollovers

401(k) money is generally intended for retirement, and withdrawals before age 59½ can trigger income taxes and an additional 10% tax unless an exception applies.

When employees leave an employer, they may have several options depending on the plan and their circumstances.

Potential choices can include:

* Leaving assets in the former employer's plan
* Rolling the balance into a Traditional IRA
* Rolling eligible assets into a new employer's retirement plan
* Converting eligible pre-tax retirement assets to a Roth account, generally creating taxable income on the converted amount
* Taking a taxable distribution

A **401(k) rollover** should be evaluated carefully because investment options, fees, creditor protections, tax treatment, and administrative considerations can differ between employer plans and IRAs.

## How to Build a 401(k) Strategy

### 1\. Review the Employer Match

Determine how much you need to contribute to receive the full employer match, if one is available.

### 2\. Choose Traditional, Roth, or Both

Compare your current tax position with your expectations for future retirement income and tax rates.

### 3\. Review Investment Options

Compare **index funds, target-date funds, stock funds, bond funds, expense ratios, and asset allocations** available through the plan.

### 4\. Automate Payroll Contributions

Because contributions are deducted directly from each paycheck, a 401(k) can make retirement investing highly systematic.

For an employee under age 50 seeking to contribute the full \$24,500 2026 elective deferral limit evenly over 12 months, the equivalent monthly amount is approximately **\$2,041.67**.

Actual payroll contributions may need to be adjusted for pay frequency, employer matching rules, plan restrictions, and annual compensation.

### 5\. Increase Contributions Over Time

Employees can consider increasing their contribution percentage after receiving raises, bonuses, or promotions.

Gradually increasing the savings rate can allow retirement contributions to rise without requiring a large immediate reduction in take-home pay.

## 401(k) Plans and Long-Term Retirement Planning

A 401(k) can serve as a central component of a broader retirement strategy, but it does not need to be the only investment account.

Employees may also use **Roth IRAs, Traditional IRAs, taxable brokerage accounts, HSAs, and other savings vehicles** depending on their eligibility and financial objectives.

The most effective structure depends on factors such as current income, tax circumstances, employer benefits, investment costs, retirement timeline, and expected future cash-flow needs.

By understanding contribution limits, employer matching, vesting, investment expenses, tax treatment, and withdrawal rules, employees can make more informed decisions about how their workplace retirement plan fits into their overall financial strategy.
