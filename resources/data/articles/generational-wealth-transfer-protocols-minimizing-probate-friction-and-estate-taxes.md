As family wealth grows, transferring assets from one generation to the next becomes more complicated than simply writing a will and naming beneficiaries. Real estate, investment accounts, business interests, life insurance, and other assets can each follow different ownership and transfer rules.

A well-structured **estate plan** can help coordinate these assets, reduce administrative friction, preserve privacy where appropriate, and address potential federal estate, gift, and generation-skipping transfer taxes.

The right strategy depends on the size and composition of the estate, the beneficiaries involved, applicable state law, and the amount of control the owner wants to retain.

## Understanding Probate and Estate Administration

**Probate** is the court-supervised process used in many jurisdictions to validate a will, identify and value assets, address creditor claims, and distribute property according to the applicable estate plan and law.

Not every asset necessarily passes through probate.

Assets held in a properly funded revocable trust, accounts with valid payable-on-death or transfer-on-death designations, certain jointly owned property, and some retirement accounts with beneficiary designations may transfer outside the probate estate.

### Why Probate Planning Matters

Probate can create administrative requirements for an estate, including:

* Filing court documents
* Identifying and valuing assets
* Notifying creditors
* Paying valid debts and taxes
* Resolving disputes
* Distributing property
* Maintaining estate records

The cost and duration of probate vary significantly by state, estate complexity, asset type, and whether disputes arise.

It is therefore misleading to assume that every probate estate costs a fixed percentage of its assets or takes a predetermined number of months.

### Privacy Considerations

Probate proceedings can involve public court filings, although the amount of information available to the public varies by jurisdiction.

A properly structured trust can provide greater privacy for assets held by the trust because the trust itself generally does not go through the same public probate process.

This can be particularly relevant for families concerned about publicly exposing detailed information about valuable property or beneficiaries.

## The 2026 Federal Estate Tax Framework

For people subject to U.S. federal estate and gift taxes, the **basic exclusion amount is \$15 million for 2026**. The same \$15 million figure applies to the federal generation-skipping transfer tax exemption for 2026\.

The federal estate-tax system generally applies a top rate of **40%** to taxable transfers above the applicable exclusion after considering deductions, credits, prior taxable gifts, and other relevant adjustments.

Importantly, having an estate worth more than \$15 million does not mean the entire estate is automatically taxed at 40%. Estate-tax calculations consider the gross estate, deductions, adjusted taxable gifts, applicable exclusion, and other provisions.

### Portability Between Spouses

A surviving spouse may potentially use the deceased spouse's unused exclusion amount through **portability**.

This generally requires the executor of the first spouse's estate to make the appropriate election on a timely filed federal estate-tax return.

The IRS notes that an estate-tax return can be required for an estate below the normal filing threshold when the executor wants to elect portability.

Consequently, married couples with significant assets should discuss portability with their estate-planning and tax advisers rather than assuming it happens automatically.

## Annual Gift Tax Exclusion

The **2026 annual gift tax exclusion is \$19,000 per recipient**. A donor can generally use the exclusion separately for each qualifying recipient. For example, gifts of \$19,000 to several different individuals can potentially qualify for the annual exclusion for each recipient.

If two eligible spouses make gifts and properly use gift splitting, the combined annual exclusion can generally reach **\$38,000 per recipient** in 2026\.

The annual exclusion is separate from the lifetime basic exclusion, but gifts exceeding the annual exclusion can require reporting and may reduce the donor's remaining lifetime exclusion without necessarily creating immediate gift tax.

### Direct Tuition and Medical Payments

Certain payments made **directly to a qualifying educational institution for tuition** or directly to a medical provider for qualifying medical expenses can receive special treatment and are generally not treated as taxable gifts for federal gift-tax purposes.

The payment must satisfy the applicable requirements. Payments made to the student or patient and later used for tuition or medical expenses generally do not receive the same treatment.

## Revocable Living Trusts and Probate Planning

A **revocable living trust** can serve as a central ownership structure for certain assets.

During the grantor's lifetime, the grantor commonly retains significant control over the trust.

After death, a successor trustee can generally administer assets held by the trust according to the trust document without requiring those assets themselves to pass through probate.

### What a Revocable Trust Does Not Automatically Accomplish

A revocable trust should not be viewed as a universal estate-tax or asset-protection structure.

Because the grantor generally retains control and beneficial enjoyment:

* Trust assets are generally still included in the grantor's taxable estate.
* The trust generally does not protect the grantor's assets from the grantor's own creditors.
* Assets never transferred into the trust may still require probate.
* Beneficiary-designation assets may follow separate transfer rules.

The trust must therefore be properly **funded and coordinated** with the rest of the estate plan.

## Irrevocable Trusts and Estate Planning

An **irrevocable trust** can be used for more specialized estate-planning purposes.

Depending on its terms and the rights retained by the grantor, transferring assets to an irrevocable trust can potentially remove those assets from the grantor's taxable estate.

However, this result is not automatic.

The tax treatment depends on factors such as:

* Trust terms
* Retained powers
* Beneficiary interests
* Timing of transfers
* Applicable federal tax rules
* State law

An irrevocable trust may also have different income-tax consequences from a revocable grantor trust.

## Irrevocable Life Insurance Trusts

An **Irrevocable Life Insurance Trust (ILIT)** can be used in certain estate plans involving substantial life-insurance policies.

Life-insurance death benefits are generally not subject to federal income tax when paid to beneficiaries, but life-insurance proceeds can be included in the insured's gross estate under certain ownership circumstances.

An appropriately structured ILIT can potentially remove life-insurance proceeds from the insured's taxable estate by placing ownership and control of the policy outside the insured's estate.

However, the trust must satisfy detailed legal and tax requirements.

Existing life insurance also requires special attention because transferring an existing policy can involve the federal **three-year rule** in certain circumstances.

Premium funding may involve gifts to the trust, beneficiary withdrawal rights, and gift-tax reporting considerations.

## Generation-Skipping Transfer Planning

The federal **generation-skipping transfer (GST) tax** applies to certain transfers benefiting individuals who are two or more generations below the transferor, as well as certain trusts treated as skip-person trusts.

For 2026, the GST exemption is **\$15 million per individual**, matching the basic estate and gift tax exclusion.

A properly structured multigenerational trust can potentially allocate GST exemption to assets intended to benefit children, grandchildren, and later generations.

The objective is not simply to eliminate every future tax. Instead, GST planning can reduce the possibility of transfer taxes arising at certain later-generation transfers.

The allocation of GST exemption and the classification of transfers can be technically complex, so the trust document and tax filings should be coordinated carefully.

## Step-by-Step Wealth Transfer Planning

### 1\. Create a Complete Asset Inventory

List:

* Bank accounts
* Brokerage accounts
* Retirement accounts
* Real estate
* Business interests
* Life insurance
* Private investments
* Valuable personal property
* Existing trusts

Record how each asset is currently titled and who is listed as beneficiary.

### 2\. Coordinate Ownership and Beneficiary Designations

Review whether each asset should be:

* Held individually
* Held jointly
* Owned by a revocable trust
* Subject to a transfer-on-death designation
* Subject to a payable-on-death designation
* Held through a business entity
* Covered by a separate irrevocable trust

A beneficiary designation can override provisions in a will for certain accounts, so the documents need to work together.

### 3\. Evaluate Federal Estate and Gift Tax Exposure

Estimate the current value of the estate and consider future appreciation.

For larger estates, calculate how lifetime gifts, prior taxable gifts, portability, and the federal exclusion could affect future estate-tax exposure.

### 4\. Consider Multigenerational Trust Structures

Families with substantial wealth may evaluate structures such as:

* Dynasty trusts
* GST trusts
* Irrevocable life insurance trusts
* Spousal trusts
* Special needs trusts
* Charitable trusts

Each structure serves different purposes and comes with its own legal, tax, and administrative requirements.

### 5\. Review the Plan Regularly

Estate plans should be revisited after significant changes such as:

* Marriage
* Divorce
* Birth or adoption
* Death of a beneficiary
* Major asset purchases
* Business sales
* Significant changes in wealth
* Relocation to another state

Tax laws and family circumstances can also change, making periodic reviews important.

## The Bottom Line

**Generational wealth transfer planning** involves more than minimizing taxes. It requires coordinating ownership, beneficiary designations, trusts, wills, insurance, business interests, and family objectives into a structure that can function when assets actually need to be transferred.

A **revocable living trust** can help with probate and administration for assets properly transferred to it, while irrevocable trusts may serve more specialized estate-tax, multigenerational, or asset-protection purposes.

For 2026, the federal basic exclusion amount is **\$15 million per individual**, while the annual gift tax exclusion remains **\$19,000 per recipient**. The GST exemption is also \$15 million for 2026\.

These figures can be important when evaluating sophisticated wealth-transfer structures, but the numerical thresholds are only one part of the analysis. The ownership of assets, trust provisions, beneficiary relationships, prior gifts, state law, and tax elections can materially change the outcome.

For substantial estates, an estate-planning attorney and qualified tax professional can coordinate the legal documents and federal tax calculations so that wealth-transfer strategies reflect both current law and the family's long-term objectives.
