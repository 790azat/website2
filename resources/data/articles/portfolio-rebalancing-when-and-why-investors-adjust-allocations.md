A portfolio can gradually become very different from the one an investor originally intended to build. A strong run in stocks may increase equity exposure, while falling bond prices or new contributions can shift the mix in another direction. Over time, these changes can alter the amount of risk an investor is taking without any deliberate decision to do so.

Portfolio rebalancing is the process of bringing investments back toward their intended allocation. It can involve selling some assets, purchasing others, redirecting new contributions, or combining several approaches to restore the desired mix.

The goal is not necessarily to predict which asset class will rise next. Instead, rebalancing is primarily a way to keep a portfolio aligned with an investor's time horizon, financial objectives, and willingness to tolerate market losses.

## What Portfolio Rebalancing Actually Changes

An asset allocation might initially divide a portfolio among stocks, bonds, and cash. An investor could, for example, establish a target allocation of 70% stocks and 30% bonds.

If stocks subsequently rise faster than bonds, the portfolio might become 78% stocks and 22% bonds. Nothing has been actively changed, but the portfolio now carries a different exposure than the original plan.

Rebalancing would move the portfolio closer to its intended allocation.

That adjustment can happen in several ways:

* Selling part of an overweight asset class
* Buying more of an underweight asset class
* Directing new deposits toward underweight investments
* Redirecting dividends and interest payments
* Adjusting contributions inside retirement accounts
* Combining purchases and sales to reduce the amount that must be sold

The mechanics can look simple, but the financial consequences depend on the account type, taxes, transaction costs, and investments involved.

## Why Allocations Drift

Market performance is the most obvious reason.

Suppose an investor owns stocks, bonds, and cash. If equities experience a prolonged rally, their percentage of the total portfolio can rise substantially. If stocks later decline while bonds remain comparatively stable, the allocation can move in the opposite direction.

Withdrawals and deposits can also create drift.

A large contribution directed entirely toward one asset class may change portfolio weights. Likewise, withdrawing money from one account or investment can leave the remaining portfolio with a different risk profile.

Changes in an investor's circumstances matter as well. Someone approaching retirement may decide that the allocation originally designed for a much longer investment horizon no longer fits their objectives.

A portfolio therefore does not need to have experienced an extreme market event to require review.

## Calendar Rebalancing Versus Threshold Rebalancing

Investors generally approach rebalancing in one of two broad ways.

**Calendar-based rebalancing** means reviewing or adjusting a portfolio at predetermined intervals, such as annually or semiannually.

The advantage is simplicity. An investor does not need to constantly monitor market movements and decide whether a small deviation requires action.

**Threshold-based rebalancing** uses a predetermined percentage deviation.

For example, an investor could establish a target of 60% stocks and 40% bonds and decide to review the allocation whenever stocks move more than five percentage points away from their target.

The distinction is important because markets can fluctuate constantly. Rebalancing after every small movement can create unnecessary trading, while never reviewing allocations can allow portfolio risk to drift substantially.

Some investors use a combination: review on a regular schedule but make adjustments only when allocations have moved beyond specified thresholds.

## Rebalancing Does Not Mean Market Timing

One common misunderstanding is that rebalancing is a method of predicting market direction.

It generally isn't.

When an investor sells an asset that has grown above its target weight and buys an asset that has fallen below its target, the transaction can feel counterintuitive. The investor is effectively reducing exposure to something that recently performed strongly and increasing exposure to something that performed relatively poorly.

That does not mean the investor knows what will happen next.

The purpose is to restore the portfolio's predetermined structure rather than to forecast the next market winner.

This distinction can make rebalancing easier to understand. It is a portfolio-management discipline, not a short-term trading strategy.

## Taxes Can Change the Rebalancing Decision

The account holding an investment can significantly affect how rebalancing is implemented.

Inside many tax-advantaged retirement accounts, selling one investment and purchasing another generally does not create a current capital-gains tax bill in the same way that selling an investment in a taxable brokerage account can.

Taxable accounts require more consideration.

Selling an investment for more than its tax basis can create a capital gain. Depending on the investment and holding period, the gain may receive different federal tax treatment.

An investor therefore might avoid selling an appreciated position unnecessarily and instead use new contributions to bring the portfolio closer to its target.

Tax-loss harvesting can sometimes be incorporated into a broader rebalancing strategy, but the rules are specific. Investors need to consider the wash-sale rules, replacement securities, account types, and the interaction between realized gains and losses.

Taxes should not automatically prevent rebalancing. They are one of the costs that should be incorporated into the decision.

## Rebalancing With New Contributions

Selling investments is not always necessary.

For investors who are regularly adding money to a portfolio, new contributions can become a useful rebalancing tool.

Imagine a portfolio with a target of 70% stocks and 30% bonds that has drifted to 65% stocks and 35% bonds. Instead of immediately selling bonds, the investor could direct new contributions primarily toward stocks until the allocation moves closer to the target.

This approach can reduce taxable sales in a brokerage account and may also lower transaction costs.

The same concept can apply to workplace retirement plans, IRAs, and other investment accounts where regular contributions are being made.

## Dividends, Interest, and Cash Flows

Portfolio income can also help with rebalancing.

Dividends from stock funds, interest from bonds, or distributions from other investments can be directed toward asset classes that have fallen below their intended allocation.

This approach can make the process more gradual.

However, investors should distinguish between receiving cash distributions and automatically reinvesting them. An automatic dividend-reinvestment instruction may purchase additional shares of the same fund, potentially increasing an already overweight position.

Reviewing where dividends and interest are going can therefore be useful when maintaining a specific allocation.

## Rebalancing Across Multiple Accounts

An investor may hold investments in several places: a 401(k), IRA, taxable brokerage account, health savings account, or other investment account.

Looking at each account independently can produce an incomplete picture.

For example, one retirement account might contain mostly bonds while a taxable brokerage account contains mostly stocks. The combined household portfolio could have a very different allocation from either account individually.

A more comprehensive approach considers the investment portfolio as a whole while also accounting for the tax characteristics and restrictions of each account.

That can allow an investor to place certain assets in accounts where they are more practical from a tax perspective while maintaining the desired overall allocation.

## Costs Matter

Every rebalancing transaction can have consequences.

Potential considerations include:

* Brokerage commissions, where applicable
* Bid-ask spreads
* Fund trading costs
* Capital-gains taxes
* Early withdrawal consequences for certain products
* Account-specific fees
* The tax implications of selling appreciated securities

Many modern brokerage platforms offer commission-free trading for certain listed securities, but that does not mean every transaction is economically costless.

Spreads, taxes, fund expenses, and market impact can still matter.

For that reason, rebalancing should be proportional to the size of the allocation drift and the portfolio's circumstances.

## Rebalancing and Risk Tolerance

The appropriate allocation depends partly on an investor's ability and willingness to withstand losses.

An investor with a long investment horizon may be comfortable holding substantial equity exposure. Someone approaching a major withdrawal period may have different liquidity and risk requirements.

Risk tolerance can also change after a significant life event.

Marriage, home purchases, career changes, inheritance, business ownership, retirement, or a change in expected spending can all affect how much investment volatility an individual can reasonably accept.

Rebalancing is therefore not always about returning to an old percentage.

Sometimes the more important question is whether the original allocation still makes sense.

If the underlying objective has changed, simply restoring the previous allocation may not address the actual problem.

## A Practical Rebalancing Review

Before making an adjustment, investors can review several questions:

**What is the current allocation?**
Calculate the actual percentage held in stocks, bonds, cash, and other major categories.

**What is the target allocation?**
Identify the intended percentages rather than relying on memory.

**How large is the deviation?**
A small difference may not justify a transaction, particularly in a taxable account.

**Why did the allocation change?**
Market performance, contributions, withdrawals, or a change in financial circumstances can produce very different decisions.

**What are the tax consequences?**
Check potential capital gains, losses, holding periods, and account-specific rules.

**Can new contributions correct the imbalance?**
If so, selling may not be necessary.

**Has the investor's objective changed?**
If the time horizon, spending needs, or risk tolerance has changed, the target allocation itself may need review.

## Rebalancing Is a Process, Not a Prediction

The most important feature of portfolio rebalancing is its relationship to discipline.

Markets can move dramatically, and a portfolio can gradually become more aggressive or more conservative without the investor explicitly choosing that change. A defined rebalancing framework creates a mechanism for reviewing those shifts.

That framework does not need to involve frequent trading. An investor might use an annual review, a percentage threshold, contribution-based adjustments, or a combination of methods.

The appropriate approach depends on the investor's circumstances, account structure, tax position, investment costs, and financial objectives.

Ultimately, rebalancing is about keeping the portfolio connected to the plan behind it. Rather than reacting to every market headline, investors can establish allocation targets, monitor meaningful deviations, consider the tax and transaction consequences, and adjust when the portfolio no longer reflects the intended level of risk.
