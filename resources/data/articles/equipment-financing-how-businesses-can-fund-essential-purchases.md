Equipment can be one of the largest capital expenses a business takes on. A contractor may need a new excavator, a manufacturer may require production machinery, a restaurant may need commercial kitchen equipment, or a medical practice may need specialized technology.

The challenge is that essential equipment can require substantial cash upfront, even though the asset may generate revenue for years.

**Equipment financing** allows a business to spread the cost of a purchase over time rather than paying the entire acquisition price immediately. Depending on the structure, financing can involve a dedicated equipment loan, a broader business loan, equipment leasing, or government-backed financing such as an SBA loan.

The right structure depends on the equipment's cost, useful life, expected revenue contribution, cash flow, ownership objectives, and tax treatment.

## What Equipment Financing Means

Equipment financing is borrowing or leasing specifically to acquire assets used in business operations.

Eligible equipment can vary widely, including:

* Manufacturing machinery
* Construction equipment
* Commercial vehicles
* Restaurant equipment
* Medical equipment
* Computers and technology
* Agricultural machinery
* Printing equipment
* Warehouse systems
* Specialized tools

A traditional equipment loan generally provides funds to purchase the asset, with the business making scheduled payments over an agreed period.

In many equipment-financing arrangements, the equipment itself serves as collateral. This can give the lender an asset it can potentially recover if the borrower defaults, although the exact collateral structure depends on the financing agreement.

The SBA notes that equipment can also be financed through several of its programs. SBA 7(a) loans, for example, can be used for purchasing and installing machinery and equipment, while the 504 program can finance certain long-term machinery and equipment.

## Why Businesses Finance Equipment Instead of Paying Cash

Paying cash eliminates financing costs, but it also removes cash from the company's operating reserves.

That distinction can matter considerably.

Suppose a company has \$300,000 in available cash and needs a \$150,000 piece of equipment. Paying cash would eliminate interest expenses, but it would also reduce immediately available liquidity by half.

If the company subsequently experiences a slow sales period, needs additional inventory, or faces an unexpected repair, that cash may have been valuable.

Financing can preserve some working capital while spreading the equipment cost over time.

The trade-off is that financing creates interest expense, fees, and a recurring payment obligation.

The decision therefore involves more than comparing the purchase price with the monthly payment.

## Matching the Loan Term to the Equipment

One of the most important considerations is the relationship between the financing period and the equipment's useful life.

A piece of heavy machinery expected to remain productive for 10 years may be financed differently from technology that could become obsolete within three years.

If the repayment period is too short, monthly payments can place unnecessary pressure on cash flow.

If the repayment period is significantly longer than the useful economic life of the asset, the company could still be making payments after the equipment has become outdated or needs replacement.

SBA rules recognize this concept in their 7(a) program. The standard maturity is generally 10 years or less unless financing equipment with a useful life exceeding 10 years, while the 504 program generally provides up to 10 years for equipment financing.

## Equipment Loans vs. Equipment Leasing

Buying and leasing equipment create different ownership and financing arrangements.

With an equipment loan, the business purchases the asset and generally owns it, subject to any lender's security interest until the debt is satisfied.

With a lease, the business pays for the right to use the equipment under the lease agreement. Depending on the lease structure, the business may have an option or obligation to purchase the equipment at the end.

The SBA notes that businesses should consider whether buying or leasing better fits their situation. Buying can provide ownership and allow the asset to appear on the balance sheet, while leasing can require less cash upfront but may involve different payment and termination provisions.

A lease should therefore be evaluated based on its full contractual cost rather than simply its advertised monthly payment.

## Calculating the Real Cost of Equipment Financing

The interest rate is important, but it is not the entire cost.

Businesses should examine:

* Interest charges
* Origination fees
* Documentation fees
* Application fees
* Closing costs
* UCC filing fees
* Appraisal or valuation expenses
* Insurance requirements
* Prepayment provisions
* Late-payment fees
* Lease acquisition or disposition fees
* End-of-term purchase requirements

For example, a financing offer with a slightly lower interest rate may still cost more overall if it includes substantial upfront or administrative charges.

A useful comparison is the **total amount paid over the financing period**.

Businesses can then compare that figure with the cash purchase price and with alternative financing structures.

## Fixed-Rate vs. Variable-Rate Equipment Financing

Some equipment loans use fixed interest rates, while others may use variable rates.

A fixed-rate loan provides greater payment predictability. The business can generally determine its scheduled principal and interest payments at the beginning of the financing period.

A variable-rate structure may initially offer a different rate but can change as the underlying benchmark changes.

For businesses with tight margins, the possibility of higher future payments deserves particular attention.

Management should calculate whether the equipment remains affordable if borrowing costs rise.

The goal is not merely to determine whether the company can make today's payment. It is to understand whether the debt remains manageable throughout the financing period.

## SBA Financing for Equipment Purchases

The SBA provides several routes that can potentially be used for business equipment.

The **7(a) program** is SBA's primary business financing program and allows proceeds to be used for purchasing and installing machinery and equipment. The maximum 7(a) loan amount is \$5 million.

The **504 program** focuses on major fixed assets. Eligible projects can include long-term machinery and equipment with a useful remaining life of at least 10 years, along with certain real estate projects. The maximum 504 loan amount is \$5.5 million.

A smaller business may also investigate the **SBA Microloan program**, which can provide loans of up to \$50,000 for eligible purposes including equipment, supplies, furniture, and fixtures.

These programs have different eligibility and underwriting requirements. SBA-backed financing is not automatically available simply because the purchase involves business equipment.

## How Lenders Evaluate Equipment Financing Applications

Lenders generally want to understand both the business and the asset being financed.

Factors can include:

* Business revenue
* Profitability
* Cash flow
* Existing debt
* Business credit
* Owner credit
* Time in business
* Industry conditions
* Equipment cost
* Equipment type
* Equipment resale value
* Expected useful life
* Down payment
* Collateral
* Intended use

The lender may also want documentation supporting the purchase, such as a vendor quote, purchase agreement, equipment specifications, or an invoice.

For newer businesses, demonstrating how the equipment will generate revenue can be particularly important.

## Equipment Should Support a Business Case

Borrowing for equipment makes more sense when the asset has a measurable operational purpose.

Consider a landscaping company purchasing a commercial mower.

The company could estimate:

* Expected additional jobs
* Revenue per job
* Fuel costs
* Maintenance
* Labor savings
* Insurance
* Financing payments
* Expected useful life

If the equipment allows the company to complete more jobs or reduce operating costs, those benefits can be incorporated into the financing analysis.

The same principle applies to manufacturing machinery, delivery vehicles, medical equipment, and other productive assets.

A business should be cautious about financing equipment simply because it appears technologically attractive. An expensive asset that sits unused still generates financing and ownership costs.

## New vs. Used Equipment Financing

Used equipment can reduce the initial purchase price, but lenders may evaluate it differently from new equipment.

Age, condition, maintenance history, expected remaining useful life, resale value, and availability of replacement parts can all affect the financing decision.

Older equipment may also have a shorter economic life, which can influence the appropriate repayment period.

A lower purchase price does not automatically mean lower total cost.

A used machine that requires frequent repairs may ultimately cost more than newer equipment with higher upfront financing requirements.

## Down Payments and Working Capital

Some financing structures require a down payment.

A down payment reduces the amount borrowed and can lower the resulting monthly payment and total interest expense.

But putting too much cash into the purchase can reduce the company's liquidity.

For this reason, management should consider the relationship between:

**Down payment \+ financing costs \+ remaining cash reserves**

rather than focusing solely on how much equity can be contributed to the purchase.

A business that exhausts its cash reserves to minimize debt may become more vulnerable to ordinary operating disruptions.

## Equipment Financing and Business Cash Flow

Equipment financing creates a recurring obligation.

Before signing, the business should add the proposed payment to its existing debt obligations and examine its cash flow under multiple scenarios.

For example:

### Expected scenario

Revenue grows as planned and the equipment reaches its expected productivity.

### Slow-growth scenario

Revenue increases more gradually than anticipated.

### Stress scenario

Revenue declines while maintenance or operating costs increase.

If the business can remain current on the debt under a reasonable range of conditions, the financing structure may be more resilient.

This type of analysis is particularly important for seasonal businesses, where cash flow can vary significantly throughout the year.

## Tax Considerations for Equipment Purchases

Equipment purchases can have tax implications beyond the financing itself.

Businesses may be able to recover the cost of qualifying equipment through depreciation or, when eligible, accelerated deductions.

For tax years beginning in **2026**, the IRS lists a maximum Section 179 expense deduction of **\$2.56 million**, with the deduction reduced when the total cost of Section 179 property placed in service exceeds **\$4.09 million**. Additional rules and eligibility requirements apply.

Tax treatment can depend on the type of property, when it is placed in service, business use, taxable income, and other circumstances.

The financing method itself does not eliminate the need to determine how the equipment should be treated for tax purposes.

Businesses should work with a qualified tax professional when significant equipment purchases are involved.

## Equipment Loans vs. Business Lines of Credit

A business line of credit can sometimes be used for equipment, but the structure differs from dedicated equipment financing.

A line of credit is generally designed for flexible borrowing. The business can draw funds as needed, subject to its credit limit and agreement.

An equipment loan is more closely tied to a specific asset and generally provides a defined amount with scheduled repayment.

A line of credit may be useful when equipment purchases are part of broader working-capital needs, while a dedicated equipment loan may provide a clearer repayment structure for a major asset purchase.

The appropriate choice depends on the company's financing needs and the terms offered by the lender.

## When Leasing May Make Sense

Leasing can be considered when a business wants access to equipment without immediately purchasing it outright.

This can be particularly relevant when technology changes rapidly.

For example, a company that depends on specialized computing hardware may prefer a structure that allows it to replace equipment periodically rather than retaining an aging asset indefinitely.

Leasing can also reduce the initial cash requirement.

However, businesses should examine the entire lease contract, including the payment schedule, maintenance responsibilities, renewal terms, early termination provisions, and any purchase option at the end of the lease.

A low monthly payment can sometimes obscure a higher cumulative cost.

## Questions to Ask Before Financing Equipment

Before committing to a financing agreement, a business owner can ask:

**What is the total amount paid over the full term?**

**Is the interest rate fixed or variable?**

**What fees are charged upfront?**

**Is a down payment required?**

**What collateral secures the financing?**

**Is a personal guarantee required?**

**What happens if the equipment is damaged or becomes obsolete?**

**Can the loan be prepaid without additional charges?**

**Does the repayment period match the equipment's useful life?**

**Would leasing produce a lower or higher total cost?**

**What tax treatment may apply to the equipment?**

**How much working capital will remain after the purchase?**

These questions help separate the economics of the equipment itself from the structure of the financing.

## A Practical Equipment Financing Example

Imagine a manufacturing company needs a \$250,000 machine.

The company has enough cash to purchase it but wants to maintain liquidity for payroll, inventory, and expansion.

Management considers financing the machine over several years.

Instead of looking only at the monthly payment, it calculates:

* Purchase price
* Down payment
* Total interest
* Financing fees
* Maintenance
* Insurance
* Expected production increase
* Additional revenue
* Expected useful life
* Tax treatment
* Remaining cash reserves

The company can then compare the financing option with paying cash or leasing the machine.

This approach evaluates the purchase as a business investment rather than simply as a monthly expense.

## Building an Equipment Financing Strategy

Equipment financing works most effectively when the debt structure reflects the economic life and business purpose of the asset.

Short-lived technology may call for a different financing approach from long-term industrial machinery. A small equipment purchase may fit within a business line of credit, while a major fixed-asset investment may justify dedicated financing or an SBA-backed structure.

Businesses should also avoid treating the lender's maximum approval amount as a recommended spending limit.

The more important question is how the equipment affects the company's cash flow after financing, maintenance, insurance, taxes, and other ownership costs are included.

Government-backed options can expand the financing choices available to eligible businesses. SBA 7(a) financing can cover machinery and equipment, while the 504 program is designed around certain major fixed assets.

Ultimately, equipment financing is a tool for converting a large capital expense into a structured financial obligation. When the asset has a clear business purpose, the repayment period is appropriate, and the company retains adequate liquidity, financing can allow a business to acquire productive equipment without committing all of its available cash at once.
