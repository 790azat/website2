Choosing an investment account involves more than deciding which stocks, funds, or other securities to purchase. The account structure determines how money can be contributed, how investment income is taxed, and how easily funds can be accessed.

Charles Schwab offers both taxable brokerage accounts and Roth IRAs, giving investors two different ways to hold investments. Although both accounts can provide access to similar securities, they serve different financial purposes.

A brokerage account emphasizes flexibility, while a Roth IRA is specifically designed for retirement savings and comes with tax advantages as well as contribution and withdrawal rules.

## Charles Schwab Brokerage Accounts

A standard Schwab brokerage account can be opened as an individual or joint account. Schwab currently lists **\$0 opening fees, \$0 maintenance fees, and no account minimums** for standard brokerage accounts.

Investors can use the account to buy and sell a range of investments, including:

* Stocks
* Exchange-traded funds
* Mutual funds
* Bonds
* Options
* Other eligible securities

One of the defining characteristics of a taxable brokerage account is that there is no IRA-style annual contribution limit. Investors can contribute additional money as their financial circumstances allow.

The account also provides relatively unrestricted access to invested funds. Schwab states that brokerage-account holders can withdraw funds without retirement-account penalties, although selling investments at a profit can create a taxable capital gain.

This makes a taxable brokerage account potentially useful for goals that do not have a specific retirement purpose.

## Charles Schwab Roth IRA

A Roth IRA is a retirement account funded with after-tax money. Contributions generally do not provide an upfront federal income-tax deduction, but qualified distributions can be tax-free.

Schwab offers Roth IRAs with **\$0 opening fees, \$0 maintenance fees, and no minimum account balance requirement**. Schwab also provides access to stocks, bonds, CDs, ETFs, and mutual funds through its IRA platform.

The Roth IRA's tax treatment is one of its primary distinctions from a standard brokerage account.

Investment growth inside the Roth IRA generally is not taxed each year as it occurs. When the account satisfies the requirements for a qualified distribution, withdrawals can generally be made without federal income tax.

However, Roth IRA rules are more restrictive than those governing a standard brokerage account. Contribution limits, income eligibility rules, and distribution requirements all need to be considered.

## 2026 Roth IRA Contribution Limits

For 2026, the combined contribution limit across an individual's traditional and Roth IRAs is **\$7,500**, or **\$8,600 for individuals age 50 or older**. Contributions are also limited by taxable compensation when that compensation is lower than the annual IRA limit.

Direct Roth IRA contributions are subject to income phase-outs.

For 2026, the phase-out ranges are:

* **\$153,000–\$168,000** for single taxpayers and heads of household
* **\$242,000–\$252,000** for married couples filing jointly
* **\$0–\$10,000** for married individuals filing separately who lived with their spouse during the year

The applicable modified adjusted gross income determines whether an investor can make the full Roth contribution, a reduced contribution, or no direct contribution.

A Schwab brokerage account does not have these Roth IRA income restrictions or the same annual contribution ceiling.

## Schwab Trading Fees and Account Costs

Schwab currently advertises **\$0 online commissions for listed U.S. stocks and ETFs**. Online options trades have a \$0 base commission plus **\$0.65 per contract**.

The same general pricing structure applies to eligible online trades in Schwab brokerage and IRA accounts.

However, investors should not interpret "\$0 commission" as meaning every investment is free.

Potential costs can include:

* Mutual fund transaction fees
* Fund expense ratios
* Options contract charges
* Broker-assisted trading fees
* Fixed-income transaction costs
* Wire or transfer fees
* Advisory fees for managed accounts

Schwab's pricing information specifically notes that other account fees, fund expenses, and brokerage commissions may apply depending on the investment or service.

For example, an ETF can have a \$0 online trading commission while still charging an annual expense ratio through the fund itself.

## Tax Treatment: Brokerage Account vs. Roth IRA

The largest difference between these accounts is how taxes interact with investment activity.

### Taxable Brokerage Account

In a standard brokerage account, taxable events can occur throughout the investment period.

Dividends and interest may be taxable in the year they are received. Selling an investment for more than its tax basis can also create a capital gain.

For example, if an investor purchases \$20,000 of stock and later sells it for \$27,000, the \$7,000 gain may be subject to capital-gains tax.

The exact tax treatment depends on factors such as the holding period, type of income, tax basis, and the investor's broader tax situation.

### Roth IRA

A Roth IRA generally moves taxation to the contribution stage rather than the withdrawal stage.

The investor contributes after-tax money, and qualifying investment growth can eventually be withdrawn tax-free.

Roth IRA contributions and earnings are treated differently under the distribution rules, so investors should not assume that every dollar withdrawn from a Roth IRA is automatically tax-free.

This distinction becomes particularly important when withdrawals occur before retirement or before the requirements for qualified distributions have been met.

## Investment Choices at Schwab

The two account types can hold many of the same types of investments.

An investor could potentially own an S\&P 500 index fund, individual stocks, bonds, or ETFs in both a taxable Schwab brokerage account and a Schwab Roth IRA.

The difference is the **tax wrapper around the investment**.

For example, suppose an investor purchases an ETF that appreciates significantly.

In a taxable brokerage account, selling the ETF at a gain can create a capital-gains tax obligation.

Inside a Roth IRA, selling an investment does not generally create a current capital-gains tax bill simply because the investment was sold within the account. The eventual withdrawal rules are what determine the tax treatment of distributions.

This can make account location an important part of long-term portfolio planning.

## Schwab's Managed Investing Options

Investors who prefer not to select and manage every investment themselves can also consider Schwab's advisory services.

One option is **Schwab Intelligent Portfolios**, which uses automated portfolio management. Schwab currently lists no advisory fee and no commissions for the service, although a **\$5,000 minimum investment** applies and the underlying ETFs have their own operating expenses.

Schwab also offers advisor-based services for investors seeking more personalized portfolio management. Those services have different minimums and fee structures.

This creates an important distinction: an investor can choose the account type separately from the method used to manage the investments.

A Roth IRA, for example, can be self-directed or used with an eligible managed-investing service.

## Access to Money

A taxable brokerage account generally provides greater flexibility for accessing funds.

There are no retirement-account age requirements governing ordinary withdrawals because the account is not itself a retirement plan. However, selling investments can create taxable gains, and withdrawing cash does not eliminate taxes that were triggered by previous investment activity.

A Roth IRA is designed for retirement, so distributions are governed by specific rules.

One important feature is that Roth IRA contributions can generally be withdrawn without being treated the same way as withdrawals of investment earnings. However, the ordering rules and requirements for tax-free treatment of earnings can become complicated.

Investors should therefore avoid treating a Roth IRA as a regular checking account simply because contributions are made with after-tax dollars.

## Using Both Accounts Together

Investors do not necessarily have to choose between a brokerage account and a Roth IRA.

The two accounts can serve different purposes within the same financial plan.

For example, an investor might contribute to a Roth IRA each year within the applicable IRS limits and use it primarily for long-term retirement assets. Additional savings could then be invested through a taxable Schwab brokerage account.

This creates two separate pools:

**Roth IRA:** Retirement-focused assets with potential for tax-free qualified distributions.

**Brokerage account:** Flexible investment assets without an IRA contribution limit or retirement-account distribution structure.

This combination can be useful for someone who wants both long-term tax-advantaged savings and investments that remain accessible for other financial objectives.

## What Investors Should Compare

Before opening or transferring an account, investors can look beyond the headline commission rate and compare the broader structure.

Important considerations include:

* Account fees and minimums
* Available investments
* Fund expense ratios
* Options and margin costs
* Tax treatment
* Contribution restrictions
* Withdrawal rules
* Advisory fees
* Research and portfolio-management tools
* Customer service and branch access
* Transfer and account-closure costs

Schwab currently provides more than 400 U.S. branches and 24/7 service, alongside its online investing platform.

For someone who values in-person assistance, that may be relevant. For another investor, the investment menu, tax structure, or portfolio-management tools may matter more.

## Building the Accounts Into a Financial Plan

A Schwab brokerage account and a Schwab Roth IRA are not simply two versions of the same investment account.

The brokerage account is primarily a flexible taxable investment vehicle. The Roth IRA is a retirement account with specific contribution and distribution rules but potentially significant long-term tax advantages.

For investors eligible to contribute to a Roth IRA, the account can provide a tax-advantaged component of a retirement portfolio. A taxable brokerage account can then provide additional investment capacity when retirement-account limits are insufficient or when money may be needed for goals outside retirement.

The decision ultimately depends on the purpose of the money, expected time horizon, tax situation, investment strategy, and need for liquidity.
