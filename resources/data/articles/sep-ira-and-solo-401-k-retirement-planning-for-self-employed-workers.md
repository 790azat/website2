Retirement planning looks different when there is no employer providing a 401(k), matching contributions, or an automatic payroll system. Freelancers, consultants, independent contractors, sole proprietors, and business owners have to create their own retirement structure—and the choice of account can have a meaningful effect on how much they can save and how contributions are taxed.

Two of the most widely used options are the **SEP IRA** and the **Solo 401(k)**. Both can accommodate substantial retirement contributions, but they work differently. A SEP IRA emphasizes simplicity and employer contributions, while a Solo 401(k) allows a business owner to contribute in both an employee and employer capacity.

Understanding those differences can make it easier to match a retirement plan with business income, cash flow, tax strategy, and whether the business may eventually hire employees.

## How a SEP IRA Works

A Simplified Employee Pension, commonly called a SEP IRA, is a retirement arrangement funded by employer contributions. A self-employed individual can establish one even when there are no other employees.

The account is essentially a traditional IRA established under a SEP arrangement, although Roth SEP IRAs are also permitted under current rules. The important distinction is that ordinary SEP contributions are employer contributions rather than employee salary deferrals.

For 2026, SEP contributions are generally limited to the lesser of **25% of eligible compensation or \$72,000**. Self-employed individuals use a special calculation to determine their own eligible compensation because self-employment tax and the contribution itself affect the calculation.

That distinction matters. A business owner generally cannot simply take a Schedule C profit figure and multiply it by 25% to determine the exact deductible contribution.

SEP plans can also be attractive for businesses with fluctuating income. An owner does not necessarily have to contribute every year. However, if a business has eligible employees and makes a SEP contribution, the contribution generally has to follow the plan's allocation rules for eligible employees as well.

## How a Solo 401(k) Works

A Solo 401(k), formally called a one-participant 401(k), is designed for a business owner with no employees other than a spouse who works in the business.

The owner effectively wears two hats: employee and employer. As the employee, the owner can make elective salary-deferral contributions. As the employer, the business can generally make an additional contribution based on compensation.

For 2026, the basic 401(k) elective-deferral limit is **\$24,500**. Participants who are at least 50 can generally make an additional **\$8,000 catch-up contribution**. A special catch-up limit of **\$11,250** applies for participants ages 60 through 63 in 2026, subject to the applicable rules.

The overall annual additions limit for a defined-contribution plan is generally **\$72,000 in 2026**, excluding catch-up contributions.

For a self-employed owner, calculating the employer contribution is more complicated than simply applying a percentage to business revenue. The IRS uses a special definition of earned income that takes into account self-employment tax and the owner's contribution.

## SEP IRA vs. Solo 401(k)

The difference becomes clearer when the two plans are viewed side by side.

| Feature | SEP IRA | Solo 401(k) |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| Primary contribution type | Employer contribution | Employee deferral \+ employer contribution |
| 2026 basic contribution ceiling | Up to \$72,000 | Up to \$72,000 before catch-up contributions |
| Employee salary deferrals | No | Yes |
| Catch-up contributions | No under SEP rules | Yes, if eligible |
| Roth option | Roth SEP may be available | Depends on plan design |
| Participant loans | Not permitted | May be available if plan permits |
| Employees | Can cover eligible employees | Generally owner and spouse only |
| Administrative complexity | Relatively low | Generally higher |
| Contribution flexibility | Strong | Strong, but more rules apply |

SEP IRAs are particularly straightforward because the business makes the contributions directly. A Solo 401(k) involves more moving parts, but those additional features can provide greater flexibility.

## Contribution Strategy Matters More Than the Account Name

Consider a self-employed consultant who earns enough to make substantial retirement contributions.

With a SEP IRA, the contribution is based primarily on the owner's eligible compensation. The owner cannot add a separate employee salary deferral on top of the SEP contribution.

A Solo 401(k), by contrast, permits the owner to make an employee deferral and then potentially add an employer contribution. That can make the Solo 401(k) particularly useful for someone with sufficient earned income who wants to maximize retirement contributions.

The difference can be meaningful even when both plans have the same overall annual contribution ceiling.

There is another important consideration for people with multiple jobs. The **\$24,500 employee elective-deferral limit for 2026 generally applies across plans on an individual basis**, rather than giving someone a separate \$24,500 employee-deferral allowance for every 401(k) they participate in.

Someone who already contributes to a traditional employer 401(k) therefore needs to account for those contributions when determining how much employee deferral room remains for a Solo 401(k).

## Taxes and Investment Growth

Traditional SEP and traditional Solo 401(k) contributions can provide tax advantages because qualifying contributions may reduce taxable income, while investment earnings generally remain tax-deferred until distributions are taken.

Solo 401(k) plans can also be designed to accept designated Roth contributions. Roth contributions are made with after-tax money rather than providing the same upfront deduction as traditional contributions, but qualified Roth distributions can generally be tax-free.

Roth SEP arrangements are also permitted under current law. The IRS notes that employers can make contributions under a SEP arrangement to either traditional SEP IRAs or Roth SEP IRAs, provided the arrangement and account satisfy the applicable requirements.

The choice between traditional and Roth treatment is therefore a separate tax-planning decision from the choice between SEP IRA and Solo 401(k).

## Business Growth Can Change the Decision

One of the most important questions is whether the business is likely to hire employees.

A Solo 401(k) is specifically structured around a business with no employees other than the owner and, potentially, the owner's spouse. If the company hires employees who meet the applicable eligibility requirements, the plan may no longer qualify as a one-participant arrangement.

A SEP IRA can accommodate employees, but that creates an important financial consideration: eligible employees generally must receive contributions under the same contribution formula used for the owner.

For a business that expects to grow from a one-person operation into a larger company, it can therefore be useful to think about retirement-plan design before hiring begins rather than treating the retirement account as a purely personal decision.

## Timing and Administration

SEP IRAs have a notable timing advantage. The IRS allows a SEP to be established as late as the due date, including extensions, of the business's federal income tax return for the year. Contributions can also generally be made by that filing deadline, including extensions.

That can be useful for a self-employed person who does not know the final year's profitability until tax preparation is underway.

Solo 401(k) plans require more attention to plan administration and contribution rules. The owner needs to establish and operate the plan according to its terms, and the calculation of self-employed contributions requires special treatment under IRS rules.

As balances grow, filing requirements can also become relevant. One-participant plans are generally exempt from Form 5500 filing while assets remain within the applicable small-plan threshold, but a Form 5500-EZ can become necessary when the plan reaches the IRS reporting threshold. Plan providers and tax professionals can help determine the filing requirements for a particular business.

## Which Structure Fits Different Self-Employed Situations?

A **SEP IRA** can make sense for an owner who prioritizes simple administration, wants flexibility around annual contributions, or expects the business to have employees and is prepared to contribute for eligible workers.

A **Solo 401(k)** can be useful for an owner without employees who wants access to employee salary deferrals, catch-up contributions, and potentially additional plan features such as Roth contributions or participant loans if the plan permits them.

Neither account automatically produces a superior retirement outcome. The appropriate structure depends on income level, business structure, employee status, desired contribution amount, tax objectives, investment preferences, and administrative tolerance.

For many self-employed workers, the decision is ultimately less about finding a single universal account and more about creating a retirement system that can continue working as business income changes.

## Building a Retirement Plan Around Variable Income

Self-employed workers often experience irregular cash flow, making retirement contributions harder to automate than they are for salaried employees.

One practical approach is to establish a contribution target based on annual profit rather than treating retirement savings as a fixed monthly bill. During stronger months, excess business cash can be reserved for retirement contributions, while weaker periods can receive a smaller allocation when the plan rules permit.

It is also useful to keep retirement contributions separate from the emergency cash needed to operate the business. A large retirement contribution may create a tax deduction, but it does not replace the working capital required for payroll, contractors, inventory, marketing, insurance, or unexpected expenses.

The strongest retirement strategy is therefore one that balances tax efficiency with business liquidity.
