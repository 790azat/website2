Commercial real estate can require substantial capital, specialized knowledge, and ongoing management. For high-income investors who want exposure to properties without purchasing and operating an entire building themselves, **real estate syndications** offer another structure.

A syndication pools money from multiple investors to acquire or operate a property or portfolio of properties. A sponsor or syndicator typically identifies the opportunity, arranges financing, oversees the transaction, and manages the asset. Investors provide capital and receive an ownership interest or another contractual economic interest according to the offering documents.

The structure can provide access to commercial properties that might otherwise be difficult for an individual investor to purchase directly. It also introduces risks that differ substantially from owning a publicly traded REIT or a property outright, including illiquidity, leverage, sponsor risk, valuation uncertainty, and potentially complex fees.

## What Is a Real Estate Syndication?

A real estate syndication is an investment structure in which multiple investors contribute capital toward a property or real estate project.

The participants generally fall into two groups:

**Sponsor or general partner:** The sponsor sources the property, arranges financing, conducts due diligence, manages the investment, and handles the property's business plan.

**Passive investors or limited partners:** Investors contribute capital and generally do not participate in the property's day-to-day management.

The exact legal structure varies. A limited liability company or limited partnership may own the property, with investors receiving membership interests or partnership interests.

This arrangement allows an investor to participate economically in a commercial property without personally negotiating leases, managing tenants, hiring contractors, or overseeing the property's daily operations.

## What Types of Properties Can Be Syndicated?

Commercial real estate syndications can cover many different property types.

Common strategies include:

* Multifamily apartment buildings
* Industrial and warehouse properties
* Office buildings
* Retail centers
* Self-storage facilities
* Hotels and hospitality properties
* Medical office buildings
* Student housing
* Mixed-use developments

The investment characteristics can vary substantially between sectors.

For example, a multifamily property may depend heavily on occupancy, rental rates, operating expenses, and local housing demand. An industrial property may rely more heavily on tenant credit quality, lease duration, logistics demand, and the property's location.

Investors should therefore evaluate the underlying real estate rather than assuming that all syndications have similar risk profiles.

## How the Investment Structure Works

A typical syndication may begin when a sponsor identifies a property requiring \$20 million of total capital.

The sponsor could arrange a combination of debt and equity. Suppose the property requires \$7 million of investor equity and \$13 million of financing.

The sponsor might then divide the equity requirement among dozens of investors.

An individual investor contributing \$100,000 could receive an ownership interest proportional to the investment structure established in the offering documents.

The property generates rental income, from which operating expenses, debt service, reserves, management costs, and other obligations are paid. Remaining distributable cash may be allocated among investors according to the partnership agreement.

At the end of the investment period, the sponsor may sell the property and distribute the proceeds according to the agreed waterfall.

The actual economics can be substantially more complicated than this simplified example.

## How Investors May Make Money

Real estate syndications can potentially generate returns through several sources.

### Rental Cash Flow

A stabilized property may generate income from tenants. After operating expenses and debt service, the remaining cash flow may be distributed to investors.

The amount and frequency of distributions depend on the property's performance and the terms of the investment.

### Property Appreciation

If the property's value increases, investors may benefit when the asset is sold or refinanced.

However, appreciation is not guaranteed. Property values can decline because of higher interest rates, weaker demand, rising expenses, changing market conditions, or property-specific problems.

### Principal Paydown

When mortgage principal is paid down using property cash flow, the equity value of the property can increase, assuming other factors remain unchanged.

This does not necessarily produce immediate cash for investors, but it can contribute to the property's equity value.

## Preferred Returns and Distribution Waterfalls

Many syndications use a distribution waterfall to determine how cash is divided between investors and the sponsor.

A structure may include a **preferred return**, which establishes a contractual priority for certain investor distributions before the sponsor receives a larger share of profits.

For example, an offering might specify an 8% preferred return before additional profits are divided according to a predetermined formula.

That does not necessarily mean the investor is guaranteed an 8% annual return.

The actual treatment depends on the agreement. A preferred return may be cumulative, non-cumulative, accruing, or subject to other conditions.

Investors should read the distribution waterfall carefully rather than treating a preferred return as equivalent to a guaranteed yield.

## Leverage and Commercial Real Estate

Debt is common in commercial real estate transactions.

Leverage can increase an investor's exposure to the property's potential gains, but it can also magnify losses.

Consider a simplified property purchased for \$10 million using \$7 million of debt and \$3 million of equity.

If the property's value increases to \$12 million, the equity value before transaction costs and other adjustments could rise substantially.

But if the property falls to \$8 million, the remaining equity can decline sharply because the debt still has to be repaid.

Interest rates also matter. Floating-rate debt can cause financing costs to rise when benchmark rates increase, while refinancing can become more expensive if a loan matures during an unfavorable credit environment.

The amount, structure, maturity, and interest-rate terms of property debt should therefore be central parts of due diligence.

## Why These Investments Can Be Illiquid

Unlike publicly traded REIT shares, private real estate syndication interests generally cannot be sold instantly on a public exchange.

An investment may have a projected holding period of several years, and the investor may have little or no ability to force an early sale.

The SEC identifies illiquidity as a major risk associated with private placements. Investors may have difficulty finding buyers and could potentially need to hold an investment indefinitely.

This makes liquidity planning especially important for high earners using syndications.

Money committed to a private property investment generally should not be money needed for near-term expenses, emergency reserves, tax obligations, or other known financial commitments.

## Accredited Investor Requirements

Many real estate syndications are structured as private securities offerings under Regulation D.

For individuals, the SEC's accredited-investor definition includes several qualification routes. One common route is having a net worth above **\$1 million, excluding the value of the primary residence**, either individually or jointly with a spouse or spousal equivalent. Another is earning more than **\$200,000 individually or \$300,000 jointly** in each of the prior two years and reasonably expecting the same income level in the current year.

Certain professional credentials and other categories can also qualify.

Importantly, being an accredited investor does not mean every private real estate investment is automatically available or appropriate. Individual offerings can impose additional minimum investments, suitability standards, or eligibility requirements.

Under Rule 506(c), an issuer may generally solicit an offering if all purchasers are accredited investors and the issuer takes reasonable steps to verify that status. Rule 506(b) generally prohibits general solicitation and can allow a limited number of qualifying non-accredited investors alongside accredited investors.

## Tax Considerations

Real estate syndications can have tax characteristics that differ from publicly traded investments.

Depending on the structure, investors may receive a Schedule K-1 reporting their share of partnership income, deductions, gains, losses, and other tax items.

Real estate ownership can also generate depreciation deductions. These deductions may reduce taxable income allocated to investors even when the underlying property produces positive cash flow.

However, the tax treatment can become complicated.

Potential issues include:

* Depreciation
* Passive-activity rules
* Capital gains
* Depreciation recapture
* State taxes
* Partnership basis
* Tax treatment of refinancing proceeds
* Tax reporting through K-1 forms

The tax benefits of a particular syndication should therefore be evaluated based on the actual property and partnership structure rather than assumed from the fact that the investment involves real estate.

## Fees and Sponsor Economics

The sponsor may receive compensation at several stages of the investment.

Potential fees include:

* Acquisition fees
* Asset-management fees
* Property-management fees
* Financing fees
* Construction or development fees
* Disposition fees
* Refinance fees
* Performance-based compensation

Some expenses are paid by the property while others may be charged at the investment-entity level.

Investors should review the offering documents to determine exactly how the sponsor is compensated and how those fees affect projected returns.

The SEC emphasizes that fees and expenses can materially reduce investment returns over time.

A projected internal rate of return should therefore be evaluated alongside the assumptions and expenses used to calculate it.

## Evaluating the Sponsor

In a passive syndication, the sponsor can have a major influence on the investment outcome.

Investors can examine:

**Track record:** What properties has the sponsor previously acquired, operated, and sold?

**Experience:** Does the team have experience with the specific property type and geographic market?

**Capital structure:** How much debt is being used?

**Alignment:** How much of the sponsor's own capital is invested?

**Communication:** How frequently does the sponsor provide financial and operational reporting?

**Prior performance:** How did previous investments perform relative to their original projections?

Past performance does not guarantee future results, but reviewing historical deals can provide context about how the sponsor handles acquisitions, operations, refinancing, and exits.

## Due Diligence on the Property

Investors should also analyze the underlying asset.

Important questions include:

* What is the property's current occupancy?
* Who are the major tenants?
* How long are the leases?
* What is the local supply of competing properties?
* What capital expenditures are expected?
* How much debt is outstanding?
* Is the debt fixed or floating rate?
* When does the loan mature?
* What assumptions drive projected rental growth?
* What happens if occupancy falls?
* What is the planned exit strategy?

Projected returns are only as reliable as the assumptions behind them.

A property whose projected performance depends on aggressive rent increases, rapid appreciation, or a highly favorable refinancing environment may carry a different risk profile from a property supported primarily by existing contractual cash flow.

## Syndication vs. REITs

Investors seeking commercial real estate exposure can also consider publicly traded REITs.

A REIT provides exposure to a portfolio of real estate assets through a publicly traded security, depending on the structure. Shares can generally be bought and sold through a brokerage account.

A private syndication provides more direct exposure to a particular property or small portfolio but usually involves significantly less liquidity.

| Feature | Private Syndication | Publicly Traded REIT |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| Investment structure | Private ownership interest | Publicly traded security |
| Liquidity | Generally limited | Generally higher |
| Property exposure | Often specific assets | Usually portfolio-based |
| Investor control | Limited | Limited |
| Valuation | Periodic estimates | Market price |
| Minimum investment | Varies | Share price |
| Disclosure | Offering-specific | Public-company reporting requirements |
| Holding period | Often several years | Investor-controlled |

Neither structure eliminates real estate risk. They simply package that exposure differently.

## Building Syndications Into a Wealth Strategy

For a high-income investor, a private commercial real estate syndication can provide exposure to rental income and property ownership without requiring direct management of the building.

But the passive nature of the investment should not be confused with low risk.

The investor is still exposed to property-market conditions, leverage, tenant risk, sponsor execution, financing conditions, valuation uncertainty, tax considerations, and limited liquidity.

Private offerings also generally provide less standardized disclosure than registered public investments. The SEC notes that private placements can involve limited disclosure and that investors may have less information available to evaluate the investment than they would for a registered offering.

For that reason, due diligence should extend beyond projected returns. Reviewing the property, sponsor, debt structure, fees, distribution waterfall, tax treatment, exit assumptions, and legal documents can help investors understand what they are actually purchasing.
