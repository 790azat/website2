Real estate investment trusts, commonly called REITs, provide a way for investors to gain exposure to income-producing real estate without purchasing and managing a property directly. Instead of buying an apartment building, office complex, warehouse, shopping center, or other property, an investor can purchase shares of a REIT through a brokerage account.

REITs can own or finance different types of real estate and may generate income from rents, leases, interest payments, or property sales. Their securities can also fluctuate significantly in value because REITs are affected by interest rates, property markets, economic conditions, financing costs, and the performance of their underlying assets.

Understanding how REITs work requires looking at more than the dividend payment. The type of real estate, structure of the REIT, balance sheet, occupancy, debt, valuation, and distribution policy can all affect an investor's experience.

## What Is a REIT?

A real estate investment trust is a company that owns, operates, or finances income-producing real estate or related assets.

Congress created the REIT structure to allow investors to participate in large-scale real estate investments without having to purchase properties individually.

Publicly traded REITs can be bought and sold through brokerage accounts in much the same way as stocks.

A REIT may specialize in a particular property sector, such as:

* Apartment buildings
* Office properties
* Retail centers
* Industrial facilities
* Warehouses
* Data centers
* Hotels
* Healthcare facilities
* Self-storage properties
* Cell towers
* Timberland

Some REITs are diversified across several property categories, while others focus heavily on one niche.

The type of property matters because different real estate sectors respond differently to economic conditions, tenant demand, interest rates, and changes in consumer behavior.

## How REITs Generate Revenue

Equity REITs generally generate revenue by owning properties and collecting rent from tenants.

For example, an apartment REIT may own thousands of rental units and collect residential rents. An industrial REIT may lease warehouses to logistics and manufacturing companies.

The REIT uses property revenue to cover operating expenses, debt costs, maintenance, taxes, and other obligations. The remaining cash can support distributions to shareholders and reinvestment into the business.

Mortgage REITs, by contrast, primarily invest in mortgages or mortgage-related securities and generate income from interest rather than directly owning rental properties.

This distinction is important because mortgage REITs can have significantly different risk characteristics from equity REITs.

## Why REITs Pay Distributions

REITs are commonly associated with dividend income because the tax rules governing REITs generally require qualifying REITs to distribute a significant portion of their taxable income to shareholders.

Under the federal REIT rules, a company generally must distribute at least 90% of its taxable income, excluding net capital gains, to shareholders in order to maintain REIT status.

This requirement helps explain why REITs can distribute substantial amounts of income.

However, investors should not assume that a high distribution yield is automatically sustainable.

A REIT can face falling rents, vacancies, rising expenses, higher interest costs, or declining property values.

The sustainability of distributions depends on the REIT's underlying business and financial position.

## REITs and Real Estate Exposure

Direct property ownership can require substantial capital.

Buying an investment property may involve a down payment, mortgage financing, property taxes, insurance, maintenance, repairs, management costs, and periods without tenants.

A publicly traded REIT offers a different structure.

An investor can purchase a relatively small number of shares and gain exposure to a portfolio of properties.

This can make REITs accessible to investors who want real estate exposure without becoming landlords.

However, publicly traded REITs are still securities. Their market prices can change every trading day even when the underlying properties have not materially changed in value.

## Publicly Traded, Non-Traded, and Private REITs

REITs are not all traded in the same way.

Publicly traded REITs are listed on securities exchanges and generally provide the greatest market liquidity.

Non-traded REITs are not listed on an exchange. They can have limited liquidity, higher fees, and different valuation mechanisms.

Private REITs are generally offered to qualified or accredited investors under applicable securities rules and may have significant restrictions on transferring or redeeming shares.

These structures should not be treated as interchangeable.

Investors considering a non-traded or private REIT should carefully review fees, redemption terms, valuation methodology, conflicts of interest, distribution policies, and offering documents.

## Equity REITs vs. Mortgage REITs

The two main categories have different business models.

### Equity REITs

Equity REITs generally own and operate income-producing properties.

Their financial performance is influenced by:

* Rental income
* Occupancy rates
* Lease terms
* Property values
* Operating costs
* Property development
* Tenant credit quality

### Mortgage REITs

Mortgage REITs generally invest in mortgages, mortgage-backed securities, or other real estate debt.

Their income is more closely tied to interest-rate movements, financing costs, credit conditions, and the spread between the income generated by assets and the cost of borrowing.

Because of these differences, an investor should understand which type of REIT they are evaluating before comparing yields or returns.

## REIT Dividends and Taxes

REIT distributions can receive different tax treatment from qualified dividends paid by many corporations.

A portion of REIT distributions may be taxed as ordinary income, while other portions can have different treatment depending on their source.

Some distributions may represent capital gains or return of capital.

Tax treatment can therefore vary from one REIT and one year to another.

Investors should review the tax information provided by the REIT and consider how REIT holdings fit within taxable brokerage accounts, Traditional IRAs, Roth IRAs, and other investment accounts.

The tax treatment of distributions should not be inferred solely from the headline dividend yield.

## Interest Rates and REITs

Interest rates can have a significant effect on REITs.

Real estate businesses frequently use debt to purchase, develop, or refinance properties. When borrowing costs rise, financing can become more expensive.

Higher interest rates can also affect property valuations and investor demand for income-producing securities.

REITs may therefore experience price pressure when interest rates rise rapidly.

The relationship is not always straightforward, however. Economic growth, rent increases, property demand, inflation, and sector-specific factors can offset or reinforce the effect of changing rates.

Investors should examine the REIT's actual debt structure rather than assuming that every REIT will respond identically to a change in interest rates.

## Evaluating REIT Debt

Debt is an important part of REIT analysis.

Investors can examine:

* Total debt
* Debt-to-assets levels
* Debt maturities
* Fixed versus variable-rate debt
* Interest coverage
* Secured versus unsecured borrowing
* Access to credit
* Refinancing requirements

A REIT with substantial debt coming due during an unfavorable financing environment may face greater pressure than one with a longer maturity schedule and more fixed-rate borrowing.

The balance sheet can therefore provide important information that a dividend yield alone does not reveal.

## Funds From Operations

Traditional earnings measures can be less informative for REITs because real estate depreciation is treated differently from the economic behavior of many properties.

Funds from operations, or FFO, is therefore widely used when analyzing REIT performance.

FFO generally starts with net income and adjusts for certain real estate-related depreciation and gains or losses from property sales.

Analysts may also examine adjusted funds from operations, commonly called AFFO, although definitions can vary among companies and analysts.

These measures can provide additional context when evaluating a REIT's ability to support distributions.

They should not be treated as identical to cash flow, however. Investors should review how the company calculates its metrics.

## Occupancy and Rental Income

For equity REITs, property-level performance is central to the investment case.

Occupancy rates indicate how much of the available property space is leased.

A REIT with declining occupancy may face weaker rental revenue and higher costs associated with vacancies and tenant turnover.

Lease terms also matter.

Long-term leases can provide greater revenue visibility, while shorter leases may allow a REIT to increase rents more quickly when market rents rise but can also expose it to more frequent tenant turnover.

The type and financial strength of tenants matter as well.

A REIT dependent on a small number of tenants may have greater concentration risk than one with a broad tenant base.

## Sector-Specific REIT Risks

Different property sectors can experience very different conditions.

Office REITs may be affected by workplace trends and corporate demand for office space.

Retail REITs can be influenced by consumer spending and competition from online commerce.

Industrial and logistics properties may depend heavily on supply chains, manufacturing activity, and distribution demand.

Data center REITs can be influenced by cloud computing and digital infrastructure demand but may also face substantial capital requirements.

Healthcare REITs can be affected by healthcare economics and the financial health of operators.

Residential REITs can be influenced by employment, household formation, housing affordability, and local rental supply.

This makes sector selection an important part of REIT investing.

## REITs and Portfolio Diversification

REITs can provide exposure to real estate within a broader portfolio of stocks, bonds, and cash.

They can also provide income and potential capital appreciation.

However, REITs are not a substitute for a diversified investment portfolio.

Publicly traded REITs can decline alongside other risk assets during periods of market stress. Investors should also consider the overlap between REIT holdings and other investments.

For example, someone who already owns significant real estate directly may have more exposure to property markets than their brokerage account alone suggests.

Looking at the entire financial position can therefore provide a clearer picture of overall real estate exposure.

## Individual REITs vs. REIT ETFs

Investors can purchase shares of individual REITs or gain diversified exposure through REIT mutual funds and ETFs.

An individual REIT allows investors to target a specific property sector or company.

A REIT ETF can spread exposure across multiple companies and property types.

The trade-off is between concentration and diversification.

An individual REIT may perform strongly if its specific properties and business model perform well, but company-specific problems can have a larger effect on the investment.

A diversified fund reduces reliance on one company but may include sectors the investor does not specifically want.

Expense ratios, holdings, sector allocations, and investment methodology should be reviewed when evaluating REIT funds.

## What to Examine Before Buying a REIT

Investors can review several characteristics before purchasing a REIT:

1. **Property type:** What real estate does the company own or finance?
2. **Occupancy:** How fully leased are its properties?
3. **Tenant concentration:** Does the company depend heavily on a small number of tenants?
4. **Lease structure:** How long do tenants generally remain under contract?
5. **Debt:** How much does the REIT borrow?
6. **Interest costs:** How sensitive is the company to changing rates?
7. **FFO and AFFO:** What do these measures indicate about operating performance?
8. **Distribution:** Is the payout supported by recurring operating performance?
9. **Valuation:** How does the share price compare with relevant financial measures?
10. **Sector outlook:** What economic conditions affect the underlying properties?

No single metric provides a complete picture.

## The Role of REITs in Long-Term Investing

REITs can give investors access to income-producing real estate without requiring direct property ownership.

They can provide exposure to rental income, commercial property, residential real estate, infrastructure, mortgages, and specialized property sectors.

But REITs remain investments with market and business risk.

Interest rates, debt levels, vacancies, tenant conditions, property valuations, economic cycles, and sector-specific changes can all affect performance.

The distribution yield is only one part of the analysis. Investors should also examine the underlying properties, balance sheet, cash generation, valuation, and structure of the REIT.

For investors building a diversified portfolio, REITs can represent one way to incorporate real estate exposure alongside stocks, bonds, cash, and other assets. The appropriate allocation depends on the investor's objectives, risk tolerance, time horizon, existing property exposure, and broader financial plan.
