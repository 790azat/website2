Taxes can have a meaningful effect on how much income and investment growth a household ultimately keeps. For investors, the tax consequences of selling appreciated assets, receiving investment income, contributing to retirement accounts, or claiming eligible deductions and credits can affect overall financial results.

Tax planning does not mean avoiding taxes illegally. It means understanding the rules that apply to your income, investments, deductions, credits, and financial accounts so that you can use available tax provisions appropriately.

Understanding the difference between **tax deductions and tax credits**, how **capital gains taxes** work, and how tax-advantaged accounts can affect investment income can make tax planning a more deliberate part of a broader financial strategy.

## Tax Deductions vs. Tax Credits

Deductions and credits both have the potential to reduce a taxpayer's federal tax burden, but they work differently.

### Tax Deductions

A **tax deduction** reduces the amount of income subject to tax.

For example, if a taxpayer in a 22% marginal federal tax bracket receives a \$3,000 deduction, the deduction can reduce taxable income by \$3,000. The resulting federal tax savings would depend on the taxpayer's circumstances and marginal tax rate; it would not generally produce a \$3,000 refund.

Taxpayers generally choose between the **standard deduction** and itemizing eligible deductions when filing their federal return.

For tax year 2026, the standard deduction is:

* **\$16,100** for single taxpayers and married individuals filing separately
* **\$24,150** for heads of household
* **\$32,200** for married couples filing jointly and qualifying surviving spouses

Itemizing can make sense when eligible deductible expenses exceed the applicable standard deduction, but the calculation depends on the taxpayer's individual circumstances.

### Tax Credits

A **tax credit** generally reduces tax liability directly rather than reducing taxable income.

For example, a \$1,000 nonrefundable federal tax credit can generally reduce a \$5,000 federal income-tax liability to \$4,000, subject to the credit's rules.

Some tax credits are **refundable**, meaning eligible taxpayers may receive a refund for some or all of the credit amount even if their regular income-tax liability is lower than the credit.

Not every tax credit is refundable, and many credits have income, filing-status, spending, or eligibility requirements.

## Capital Gains Taxes

Investment gains in a taxable brokerage account can create a tax liability when assets are sold for more than their adjusted basis.

The tax treatment generally depends on how long the asset was held.

### Short-Term Capital Gains

An asset generally produces a **short-term capital gain** when it is held for one year or less before being sold for a gain.

Short-term capital gains are generally taxed at ordinary federal income-tax rates rather than the preferential long-term capital-gains rates.

The difference can be significant for investors who frequently buy and sell investments in taxable accounts.

### Long-Term Capital Gains

An investment generally qualifies for long-term capital-gain treatment when it is held for **more than one year** before being sold.

For many taxpayers, long-term capital gains receive preferential federal tax rates. The applicable rates can be 0%, 15%, or 20%, depending on taxable income and filing status. Certain types of gains, including some collectibles and unrecaptured Section 1250 gain, can be subject to different maximum rates.

The tax benefit of holding an investment for more than one year should therefore be considered before selling an appreciated asset in a taxable brokerage account.

## Tax-Loss Harvesting

**Tax-loss harvesting** involves selling an investment that has declined in value to realize a capital loss.

Those losses can generally offset capital gains. If total capital losses exceed capital gains, an individual generally can deduct up to **\$3,000** of net capital loss against other income in a year, or \$1,500 for a married individual filing separately. Unused losses can generally be carried forward to future years.

However, investors should not treat every loss sale as automatically deductible.

### The Wash-Sale Rule

The **wash-sale rule** can limit the tax benefit when an investor sells a security at a loss and acquires substantially identical securities within the applicable 30-day period before or after the sale.

This means tax-loss harvesting requires attention to transaction dates and replacement investments.

Investors should also consider trading costs, portfolio allocation, market exposure, and the economic reason for selling rather than making a transaction solely for a potential tax deduction.

## Tax-Advantaged Retirement Accounts

Retirement accounts can change when and how investment income is taxed.

### Traditional 401(k) and IRA Accounts

Contributions to a traditional 401(k) can generally receive favorable tax treatment under applicable employer-plan rules.

Contributions to a traditional IRA may be deductible, but the deduction can depend on income, filing status, and whether the taxpayer or spouse is covered by a retirement plan at work.

For 2026, the combined contribution limit across traditional and Roth IRAs is generally **\$7,500**, with an \$8,600 catch-up contribution limit for eligible individuals age 50 or older.

Traditional retirement accounts generally defer taxation on investment earnings until distributions are taken, subject to applicable rules.

### Roth IRAs

Roth IRA contributions are generally made with after-tax dollars rather than providing a current federal income-tax deduction. Qualified distributions can generally be taken tax-free if applicable requirements are satisfied.

Income limits and other rules apply to Roth IRA contributions.

For that reason, the choice between traditional and Roth contributions involves both current and future tax considerations.

## Asset Location and Tax Efficiency

Investors with taxable brokerage accounts and retirement accounts may also consider **asset location**—the process of deciding which types of investments to hold in different account types.

Some investments generate relatively high levels of taxable interest or distributions. Others may generate fewer taxable distributions.

Holding investments in tax-advantaged accounts can potentially defer or eliminate certain forms of current taxation, depending on the account.

However, there is no universal asset-location strategy. Investors should consider expected returns, income characteristics, withdrawal plans, tax brackets, account restrictions, and investment objectives.

## Charitable Giving and Tax Planning

Charitable contributions can potentially provide tax benefits for eligible taxpayers who itemize deductions and meet the applicable requirements.

For investors holding appreciated securities, donating eligible appreciated assets can have different tax consequences from selling the asset first and donating cash.

Other strategies, including qualified charitable distributions from eligible retirement accounts, have their own age, account, and annual-limit requirements.

Because charitable tax rules can be complex, taxpayers should verify the applicable rules for the tax year in which the contribution is made.

## Capital Gains and Investment Timing

Selling an appreciated investment can create a taxable event even when the proceeds are immediately reinvested.

Before selling, consider:

* Purchase date
* Cost basis
* Unrealized gain
* Expected taxable income
* Filing status
* Long-term versus short-term holding period
* Available capital losses
* State income-tax treatment
* Whether the investment remains appropriate for the portfolio

In some circumstances, an investor who is only a short time away from crossing the one-year holding period may want to evaluate the tax consequences of selling immediately versus waiting. The investment risk of continuing to hold the asset should also be considered.

Tax considerations should inform investment decisions rather than override them automatically.

## How to Organize a Tax-Efficient Investment Strategy

A practical tax-planning process can begin with several steps.

### 1\. Review Your Filing Position

Determine your filing status, taxable income, standard deduction, and whether itemizing deductions could apply to your situation.

### 2\. Review Retirement Contributions

Check whether you are maximizing available employer-plan contributions or eligible IRA contributions based on your financial circumstances and applicable limits.

### 3\. Audit Taxable Investments

Review unrealized gains and losses, cost basis, holding periods, and expected distributions.

### 4\. Identify Potential Capital Losses

Determine whether realizing losses could offset existing capital gains, while accounting for wash-sale restrictions and the investment implications of selling.

### 5\. Review Major Transactions Before Year-End

Selling investments, exercising certain equity compensation, realizing business income, making large charitable contributions, or completing other major transactions can affect taxable income.

Planning before the transaction occurs generally provides more flexibility than trying to correct the tax consequences afterward.

### 6\. Keep Accurate Records

Maintain records of purchase prices, reinvested dividends, capital improvements where relevant, charitable contributions, retirement contributions, and investment sales.

Accurate cost-basis information is particularly important when calculating taxable investment gains and losses.

## The Bottom Line

**Tax deductions, tax credits, and capital gains rules** can all influence how much income and investment growth a household ultimately retains.

Deductions reduce taxable income, while credits generally reduce tax liability directly. Capital gains receive different treatment depending on the holding period and the taxpayer's overall taxable income. Tax-loss harvesting can help offset gains, but wash-sale rules and capital-loss limitations must be considered.

Retirement accounts, charitable giving, asset location, and the timing of investment sales can also affect a household's tax position.

Rather than focusing exclusively on investment returns before taxes, incorporate **tax-efficient investing, retirement contributions, capital gains planning, and available deductions and credits** into the broader financial plan. Because tax rules can change and individual circumstances vary, significant transactions should be reviewed using current IRS guidance and, when appropriate, with a qualified tax professional.
