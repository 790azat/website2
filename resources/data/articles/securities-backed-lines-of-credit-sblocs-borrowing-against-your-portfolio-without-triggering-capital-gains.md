Selling appreciated investments can create a tax bill at exactly the moment an investor needs liquidity. For someone holding a large portfolio of stocks, bonds, or mutual funds with substantial unrealized gains, an alternative is to borrow against those assets rather than sell them.

A **securities-backed line of credit (SBLOC)** allows an investor to pledge eligible securities as collateral for a revolving line of credit. Because the investor does not sell the pledged investments simply by taking the loan, borrowing itself generally does not create a capital gain.

That feature can make SBLOCs useful for certain high-net-worth investors, but the strategy comes with an important trade-off: the portfolio becomes collateral for a variable-rate loan, and a significant market decline can create a requirement to provide additional collateral or repay part of the balance.

## What Is a Securities-Backed Line of Credit?

An SBLOC is a revolving loan secured by an investment portfolio. The lender determines which securities are eligible and assigns an advance rate to them. The amount available to borrow therefore depends on both the portfolio's market value and its composition.

Eligible collateral may include securities such as publicly traded stocks, bonds, and mutual funds, although requirements vary between lenders.

Unlike a margin loan, an SBLOC is generally a **non-purpose loan**, meaning the proceeds cannot be used to purchase or trade securities. The SEC and FINRA have noted that SBLOC proceeds can generally be used for other purposes, including expenses such as home renovations, education, business needs, or tax payments.

The borrower normally makes interest payments while the principal remains outstanding. Because it is a revolving facility, principal can generally be repaid and borrowed again within the approved credit limit.

## Why Borrowing Can Avoid an Immediate Capital Gain

The tax distinction is straightforward.

Suppose an investor owns \$2 million of stock with a \$500,000 cost basis. Selling the entire position could create a substantial realized capital gain.

Borrowing \$500,000 against the portfolio is different. The investor has borrowed money rather than disposed of the securities, so the loan proceeds themselves are generally not taxable income.

The investor continues to own the underlying investments and can potentially continue receiving dividends, interest, and appreciation.

This can be particularly relevant when an investor wants liquidity but does not want to sell a concentrated position immediately.

However, **avoiding a taxable sale is not the same as eliminating the eventual tax liability**. If the securities are eventually sold, the applicable gain or loss is generally determined at that time. And if the lender liquidates securities because collateral requirements are no longer satisfied, that sale can create taxable consequences.

The SEC specifically warns that forced liquidation of pledged securities can potentially result in capital-gains taxes depending on the investor's basis and circumstances.

## How Much Can You Borrow?

There is no universal SBLOC advance rate.

The lender evaluates the securities in the account and determines how much of their value can support borrowing. More volatile or concentrated securities may receive lower advance rates than assets considered more stable or liquid.

For example, an investor with a \$1 million portfolio might not have access to \$1 million of credit. The lender could establish a substantially lower borrowing limit based on the portfolio's composition.

The SEC's investor guidance has historically described SBLOC credit limits ranging from roughly 50% to 95% of eligible assets, depending on the securities involved and the lender's requirements. These figures are illustrative rather than a current universal lending standard; actual terms vary by institution.

Investors should also distinguish between the **maximum amount available** and the amount that can be borrowed comfortably. Using the full credit line can leave substantially less room to absorb a market decline.

## The Main Risk: A Falling Portfolio

The central risk of an SBLOC is that the value of the collateral can fall while the loan balance remains outstanding.

Imagine an investor has:

* \$1,000,000 of eligible securities
* \$300,000 outstanding on an SBLOC
* A lender requirement that the collateral remain above a particular threshold

If the portfolio falls sharply, the investor's borrowing balance does not automatically fall with it. The loan can therefore become too large relative to the collateral.

The lender may issue a **maintenance call**, requiring the borrower to provide additional eligible assets or repay part of the outstanding balance.

According to Investor.gov, borrowers may have only a short period to address such a shortfall. If they cannot provide sufficient collateral or repayment, the lender may liquidate securities to satisfy the requirement.

This creates a potentially uncomfortable sequence: markets fall, collateral value declines, and the investor may be forced to sell investments when prices are already depressed.

## Interest Rates Matter More Than the Tax Savings

An SBLOC can defer a capital-gains realization, but the loan itself has a cost.

Interest rates are generally variable and can change over time. The exact pricing depends on the lender, credit arrangement, portfolio, loan size, and prevailing market conditions.

Consider a \$500,000 balance at a hypothetical 7% annual interest rate. The interest expense would be approximately \$35,000 for a full year before considering changes in the balance or rate.

If the portfolio produces dividends or other investment income, those cash flows may help offset the borrowing cost. But there is no guarantee that investment returns will exceed the interest rate.

An investor should therefore evaluate an SBLOC based on the **total cost of borrowing**, not simply the fact that the loan avoids an immediate taxable sale.

## Is the Interest Tax-Deductible?

The tax treatment of SBLOC interest depends substantially on **how the borrowed money is used**.

The IRS states that personal interest generally is not deductible. Interest on money borrowed for investment purposes may qualify as investment interest, subject to applicable rules and limitations. The IRS also requires debt to be allocated among personal, business, and investment uses.

For example, if a borrower uses part of an SBLOC for an investment purpose and part for personal expenses, the interest cannot simply be treated as entirely investment interest.

This makes recordkeeping particularly important. The fact that securities were used as collateral does not by itself determine the tax treatment of the interest.

Investors considering a large SBLOC should discuss the intended use of proceeds with a tax professional before borrowing.

## SBLOC vs. Selling Investments

The choice is essentially a trade-off between immediate taxation and ongoing leverage.

| Consideration | Sell Securities | Use an SBLOC |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| Immediate capital gain | Potentially yes | Generally no from the borrowing itself |
| Portfolio ownership | Reduced | Retained |
| Interest expense | None | Yes |
| Collateral risk | None from the loan | Yes |
| Market exposure | Reduced after sale | Generally remains |
| Forced liquidation risk | No loan-related risk | Possible if collateral requirements are breached |
| Flexibility | Cash is unrestricted | Loan proceeds subject to lender terms |

Neither approach automatically makes sense in every situation.

Selling can reduce investment risk and eliminate debt. Borrowing can preserve the investment position and defer a taxable sale, but introduces leverage and interest costs.

## Where SBLOCs Can Fit Into Wealth Planning

An SBLOC is generally more relevant when an investor has substantial liquid assets and a temporary or strategic need for cash.

Potential uses include:

* Funding a major purchase without immediately selling investments
* Providing short-term liquidity for a business
* Financing certain real estate transactions
* Covering large tax obligations
* Funding education or other major expenses
* Bridging a liquidity gap while another asset is being sold

For example, an investor selling a business might need several months of liquidity before receiving the proceeds from the transaction. An SBLOC could potentially provide temporary financing while the underlying portfolio remains invested.

The strategy becomes more complicated when the loan is intended to remain outstanding indefinitely.

## Concentrated Portfolios Require Extra Caution

A portfolio dominated by one company's stock can create a particularly difficult collateral situation.

Suppose an executive owns \$5 million of employer stock and borrows \$2 million against it. A major decline in that company's share price could simultaneously reduce the investor's net worth and weaken the collateral supporting the loan.

The investor may then face pressure to sell the same asset that caused the collateral problem.

FINRA has highlighted the risks associated with concentrated collateral, including the possibility that borrowers may be forced to liquidate securities after significant declines.

Diversification does not eliminate SBLOC risk, but a more diversified collateral pool may provide greater flexibility when individual securities decline.

## Questions to Ask Before Opening an SBLOC

Before signing an agreement, an investor should understand:

* What securities qualify as collateral?
* What advance rates apply to each asset?
* What triggers a maintenance call?
* How much time is provided to satisfy a call?
* Can the lender change collateral requirements?
* How quickly can pledged securities be liquidated?
* Is the interest rate fixed or variable?
* What fees apply?
* What happens if a security becomes ineligible collateral?
* Can the lender demand repayment?
* What restrictions apply to transferring the portfolio?
* Who is actually providing the loan?

Investor.gov specifically recommends understanding who the lender is, how collateral requirements work, how interest rates can change, and what happens if portfolio values decline.

## The Strategy Works Through Leverage, Not a Tax Loophole

The appeal of an SBLOC is easy to understand: an investor can access liquidity without necessarily selling appreciated securities and triggering an immediate capital gain.

But the tax advantage should not obscure the underlying economics.

The investor is exchanging a potential taxable sale for a loan secured by volatile assets. Interest continues to accrue, collateral requirements can change, and a severe market decline can force a sale at an unfavorable time.

For investors with substantial portfolios, the appropriate question is therefore not simply whether an SBLOC can avoid capital gains today. It is whether the **cost, leverage, collateral requirements, tax treatment, and repayment plan** make sense relative to selling some investments and paying the associated tax.

Used within a carefully designed liquidity plan, an SBLOC can provide flexibility without immediately disrupting a long-term portfolio. Used without sufficient collateral reserves or a realistic repayment strategy, the same structure can turn a temporary cash need into a significant portfolio risk.
