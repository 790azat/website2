Investors who want broad market exposure do not necessarily have to purchase a single index fund or ETF. Another approach, known as **direct indexing**, involves owning many of the individual securities that make up an index and managing those holdings as a customized portfolio.

The strategy has gained attention among investors with taxable assets because individual securities can potentially be managed for tax-loss harvesting, exclusions, and other personalized preferences. Direct indexing can also allow investors to avoid or reduce exposure to particular companies, industries, or business activities.

The trade-off is greater complexity. Instead of owning one fund that handles the underlying portfolio, the investor—or an investment manager—must manage a collection of individual securities.

## What Is Direct Indexing?

Traditional index investing generally involves purchasing a mutual fund or ETF designed to track an index.

For example, an investor seeking exposure to the S\&P 500 could purchase an index fund that owns shares of the companies represented in that benchmark.

With direct indexing, the investor owns individual securities that are selected to approximate the performance and characteristics of the chosen index.

The portfolio may contain hundreds of individual stocks rather than one fund.

The objective is generally to achieve **index-like exposure while retaining greater control over the underlying securities**.

That control creates opportunities that are difficult or impossible to implement directly inside a conventional index fund.

## How the Strategy Works

A direct-indexing portfolio begins with a benchmark.

The investment manager or software platform then constructs a portfolio of individual securities intended to track that benchmark within an acceptable level of deviation.

The portfolio may not own every constituent of the index. Instead, an optimization process can determine which securities to hold and in what amounts.

As market prices change, the portfolio can be rebalanced to maintain its intended exposure.

This means direct indexing involves ongoing portfolio management rather than simply buying an index fund and leaving it untouched.

The degree of customization can vary substantially between providers.

## Tax-Loss Harvesting

One of the main reasons investors consider direct indexing is the ability to manage gains and losses at the individual-stock level.

Suppose a portfolio contains 300 stocks. During a market decline, several positions may fall below their purchase prices while the broader portfolio remains relatively stable.

An investor may be able to sell some securities that have declined, realize capital losses, and replace them with securities that maintain similar—but not identical—market exposure.

Those realized losses can potentially be used to offset capital gains, subject to applicable tax rules.

The IRS generally permits capital losses to offset capital gains. If losses exceed gains, individuals can generally deduct up to **\$3,000 of net capital loss against other income** in a tax year, with unused losses generally carried forward to future years. ([irs.gov](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc409))

Direct indexing creates more opportunities for this type of tax management because each individual security has its own cost basis.

An ETF, by contrast, does not generally allow the investor to sell individual underlying stocks within the fund.

## The Wash-Sale Rule

Tax-loss harvesting needs to be handled carefully because selling an investment at a loss and immediately purchasing a substantially identical security can trigger the **wash-sale rule**.

Under the federal tax rules, a loss may be disallowed when an investor sells or trades stock or securities at a loss and acquires substantially identical stock or securities within the 30-day period before or after the sale. The rule can also apply in certain situations involving a spouse or an IRA. ([irs.gov](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p550))

Direct indexing platforms typically use rules-based systems to monitor transactions and attempt to avoid wash-sale problems.

However, investors with multiple brokerage accounts need to consider the portfolio as a whole. A tax-loss harvest in one account can potentially interact with purchases in another account.

This makes accurate recordkeeping and coordination particularly important.

## Customizing a Portfolio Around Personal Values

Direct indexing can also provide more control over which companies are included in a portfolio.

An investor may want to reduce or eliminate exposure to companies involved in particular industries or business activities.

Potential screening criteria might include:

* Fossil-fuel production
* Tobacco
* Firearms
* Gambling
* Certain environmental practices
* Specific labor practices
* Corporate governance characteristics

Instead of removing an entire sector through a broad exclusion fund, direct indexing can allow more precise security-level decisions.

For example, an investor may want broad exposure to the technology sector while excluding a small number of companies based on predetermined criteria.

This level of customization is difficult to achieve when owning a conventional index fund containing all or most of the benchmark's constituents.

## Customization and Portfolio Drift

There is an important trade-off between personalization and index tracking.

The more securities an investor removes from an index, the greater the potential difference between the customized portfolio and the original benchmark.

Suppose an investor excludes several large companies from an index. If those companies subsequently outperform the broader market, the customized portfolio may lag the benchmark.

That does not necessarily mean the strategy failed. The investor intentionally accepted tracking differences in exchange for customization.

The relevant question is whether the deviations are consistent with the investor's objectives.

## Direct Indexing vs. ETFs

The distinction becomes clearer when the two structures are compared.

| Feature | Direct Indexing | Index ETF |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| Ownership | Individual securities | Fund shares |
| Customization | High | Limited |
| Tax-loss harvesting | Security-level opportunities | More limited |
| Portfolio management | Ongoing | Fund manager handles holdings |
| Exclusions | Highly customizable | Depends on fund methodology |
| Tracking difference | Can vary based on customization | Usually designed to closely track benchmark |
| Minimum investment | Varies by provider | Often relatively low |
| Complexity | Higher | Lower |

An ETF can provide broad diversification with relatively little management effort.

Direct indexing requires more portfolio construction and monitoring but provides additional control.

## Where Direct Indexing May Fit

The strategy is particularly relevant for investors with substantial taxable portfolios.

Tax-loss harvesting becomes more meaningful when there are enough individual positions and realized gains for harvested losses to have a material tax effect.

Investors may also consider direct indexing when they have concentrated positions that need to be reduced gradually, significant unrealized gains, or specific portfolio exclusions.

For someone investing a relatively small amount of money in a retirement account, the additional complexity may provide fewer practical benefits.

That is because tax-loss harvesting is generally most relevant in taxable accounts. Retirement accounts do not receive the same tax treatment for capital gains and losses.

## Concentrated Stock Positions

Direct indexing can also be used alongside concentrated-stock management.

Imagine an investor who receives substantial shares of a public company through employment or business ownership.

Selling the entire position immediately could create a large taxable gain. A broader portfolio can potentially be constructed around the existing position while gradually reducing the concentration over time.

Direct indexing does not eliminate the tax consequences of selling appreciated shares, but it can give an investor more control over the rest of the taxable portfolio.

An advisor may also use tax-loss harvesting elsewhere in the portfolio to help manage realized gains, subject to the applicable tax rules.

## Tax-Loss Harvesting Is Not Free

A tax loss is not equivalent to free money.

When an investor sells an asset at a loss, the investment exposure needs to be considered. If the replacement investment behaves differently from the original holding, the portfolio can temporarily deviate from the intended strategy.

There may also be trading costs, bid-ask spreads, management fees, and other expenses.

The tax benefit also depends on the investor actually having gains or other income against which the loss can be used.

For these reasons, tax-loss harvesting should be viewed as one component of portfolio management rather than the sole justification for adopting direct indexing.

## Costs and Minimum Investment Requirements

Direct indexing is generally more complicated to operate than buying a single ETF.

Some providers use automated portfolio-management systems to handle hundreds of individual positions, tax-loss harvesting, rebalancing, and customization.

Those services can involve advisory fees.

Investors should compare the total cost with the expenses of a conventional index fund or ETF.

Relevant costs can include:

* Advisory fees
* Trading costs
* Bid-ask spreads
* Account fees
* Tax-management service fees
* Underlying investment expenses, where applicable

The value of the strategy depends partly on whether the expected tax and customization benefits justify those additional costs.

## Direct Indexing in Retirement Accounts

Direct indexing is generally more compelling in taxable brokerage accounts than in tax-advantaged retirement accounts.

The main reason is taxation.

In a taxable account, investors can potentially realize capital losses and manage individual tax lots. In a Roth IRA or traditional IRA, gains and losses generally do not receive the same annual tax treatment.

This does not mean direct indexing cannot be used in retirement accounts. It means one of the strategy's major potential advantages—tax-loss harvesting—is primarily relevant to taxable assets.

Investors should therefore evaluate direct indexing account by account.

## Measuring Tracking Error

Direct indexing does not necessarily produce the exact return of the underlying index.

The difference between the portfolio's performance and the benchmark's performance is commonly referred to as **tracking error** or tracking difference, depending on the measurement.

Several factors can contribute:

* Excluded securities
* Tax-loss harvesting
* Cash balances
* Rebalancing
* Transaction costs
* Portfolio optimization
* Corporate actions
* Custom restrictions

A direct-indexing portfolio should therefore be evaluated against the investor's actual objectives rather than assuming that matching the benchmark perfectly is always the goal.

## Building a Direct-Indexing Strategy

A practical direct-indexing process can begin with several decisions.

**Choose the benchmark.**
Determine whether the portfolio is intended to represent the U.S. market, a particular large-cap index, international equities, or another segment.

**Define exclusions.**
Establish which companies or industries should be restricted and why.

**Set a tracking range.**
Decide how much deviation from the benchmark is acceptable.

**Establish tax priorities.**
Determine whether tax-loss harvesting, gain management, or charitable transfers are important objectives.

**Coordinate accounts.**
Review holdings across taxable and retirement accounts to avoid unintended concentration or wash-sale issues.

**Review costs.**
Compare advisory and trading expenses with those of a conventional index fund.

This framework keeps customization connected to the overall investment strategy.

## The Role of Direct Indexing in Wealth Management

Direct indexing combines the broad-market philosophy of index investing with individual-security ownership.

Its primary appeal is control. Investors can potentially customize which companies they own, manage individual tax lots, harvest losses, and adjust the portfolio around existing holdings.

But those advantages introduce complexity.

The strategy requires ongoing monitoring, careful tax management, and an understanding of how customization affects portfolio performance relative to the benchmark.

For investors with straightforward portfolios, a diversified ETF may provide the desired market exposure with less administrative work. For investors with larger taxable portfolios, substantial capital gains, concentrated positions, or specific customization requirements, direct indexing can provide additional planning tools.

The key is to evaluate the strategy based on its actual tax, investment, and portfolio-management benefits rather than assuming that more customization automatically produces better results.
