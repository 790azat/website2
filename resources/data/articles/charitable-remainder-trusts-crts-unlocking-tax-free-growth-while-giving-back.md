For investors with highly appreciated assets, charitable giving can create an interesting planning challenge. Selling appreciated stock, real estate, or another investment may generate a substantial capital-gains tax bill, while simply donating the asset can mean giving up the opportunity to receive income from it.

A **charitable remainder trust (CRT)** is designed to address both objectives. It allows an individual to transfer assets into an irrevocable trust, receive payments from the trust for life or a specified period, and ultimately direct the remaining assets to a qualifying charity. The structure can also defer recognition of capital gains when appreciated assets are sold inside the trust.

The phrase “tax-free growth” needs some qualification, however. A CRT is generally exempt from federal income tax at the trust level, but distributions to the noncharitable beneficiaries can carry taxable income. The tax treatment depends on the character of the trust's income and gains.

## How a Charitable Remainder Trust Works

A CRT is an **irrevocable split-interest trust**. The arrangement has two beneficiaries:

* A noncharitable beneficiary receives income payments during the trust term.
* A qualified charitable organization receives whatever remains when that income interest ends.

The trust can last for the lifetime of one or more beneficiaries or for a specified period of up to 20 years. The charitable remainder interest must generally represent at least 10% of the initial fair market value of the property transferred to the trust.

Consider an investor who owns \$1 million of stock with a \$200,000 tax basis. Selling the shares directly could create a significant capital-gains liability.

Instead, the investor could contribute the shares to a properly structured CRT. The trust takes the asset with its carryover basis, sells the investment, and reinvests the proceeds. The CRT itself generally does not pay ordinary federal income tax or capital-gains tax in the same manner as an individual investor immediately recognizing the sale.

That does **not** mean the gain disappears. Instead, the tax consequences are generally reflected through the distributions eventually made to the beneficiary.

This distinction is important when evaluating whether a CRT actually improves a financial plan.

## CRAT vs. CRUT

There are two primary forms of charitable remainder trusts: the **charitable remainder annuity trust (CRAT)** and the **charitable remainder unitrust (CRUT)**.

### Charitable Remainder Annuity Trust

A CRAT pays a predetermined amount to its beneficiary each year. The payment is based on the initial value of the assets contributed to the trust.

Federal rules generally require the annuity percentage to be at least 5% and no more than 50%, while additional requirements apply to the projected charitable remainder.

The fixed-payment structure can make a CRAT easier to understand from a cash-flow perspective, but it also means the payment does not automatically increase when the trust's investments appreciate.

### Charitable Remainder Unitrust

A CRUT instead pays a specified percentage of the trust's assets, with the assets generally valued annually.

If the trust grows, future payments can increase. If the portfolio declines, payments can decline as well.

Some CRUTs can also include provisions that coordinate distributions with the trust's actual income. These structures can become considerably more complicated and require careful drafting and administration.

For investors considering a CRT, the CRAT-versus-CRUT decision should therefore be evaluated alongside expected cash-flow needs, investment objectives, asset characteristics, and the intended charitable gift.

## Where the Tax Benefits Come From

One of the primary attractions of a CRT is the ability to contribute appreciated property without necessarily triggering an immediate personal capital-gains tax bill on the transfer.

Suppose an investor has \$2 million of highly appreciated shares and wants to diversify but does not want the entire gain recognized immediately.

A CRT may allow the trust to sell the contributed shares and reinvest the proceeds without the individual personally recognizing the entire gain at the time of the trust's sale.

The result can create more capital available for continued investment inside the trust.

The investor may also qualify for a **partial charitable deduction** based on the present value of the charitable remainder interest. The deduction is not equal to the entire amount contributed because the donor retains an income interest. IRS rules also impose applicable deduction limitations.

This combination—potential gain deferral, continued income, and a partial charitable deduction—is what makes CRTs particularly relevant to sophisticated charitable and estate-planning strategies.

## CRT Distributions Are Not Automatically Tax-Free

This is one of the most important points for anyone evaluating a CRT.

Payments received by a noncharitable beneficiary are generally reported on Schedule K-1 and follow a specific tax-character ordering system.

The IRS generally describes the ordering as:

1. Ordinary income
2. Capital gains
3. Other income, including certain tax-exempt income
4. Trust corpus or principal

Consequently, a beneficiary receiving payments from a CRT may owe income tax on some or all of the distributions, depending on the trust's accumulated income and gains.

The structure is therefore better understood as a **tax-deferral and charitable-planning strategy** rather than a mechanism that permanently eliminates income tax.

That distinction matters when comparing a CRT with simply selling an investment and paying the resulting tax.

## Which Assets Can Make Sense for a CRT?

CRTs are often considered when an investor owns assets with substantial unrealized appreciation.

Potential candidates can include:

* Appreciated publicly traded securities
* Certain real estate interests
* Closely held business interests
* Other qualifying investment property

The suitability of a particular asset depends heavily on its liquidity, valuation, transfer restrictions, basis, and the trust's ability to sell and manage it.

Publicly traded securities are generally easier to value and transfer than a privately held business interest. Real estate may also require additional analysis involving financing, valuation, depreciation, and unrelated business considerations.

A CRT should therefore be designed around the asset rather than treated as a generic container for investments.

## The Charitable Deduction Has a Specific Value

Contributing \$1 million to a CRT does not necessarily produce a \$1 million charitable deduction.

The deduction is generally based on the **present value of the charity's projected remainder interest**. Factors such as the amount paid to beneficiaries, the trust term, beneficiary ages, asset value, and applicable actuarial assumptions influence that calculation.

This creates an important planning trade-off.

A larger income interest generally leaves less value for the charity, while a larger projected charitable remainder can increase the charitable deduction.

The trust must be modeled carefully before assets are transferred because the decision is generally irreversible.

## Estate and Family Planning Considerations

CRTs can also play a role in broader wealth-transfer planning.

For example, a donor might establish a CRT that provides income to the donor for life and then to a spouse or another permitted beneficiary, with the remaining assets ultimately passing to a charitable organization.

However, CRTs are not simply estate-planning substitutes for revocable trusts, irrevocable family trusts, or other wealth-transfer structures.

Once assets enter the CRT, the donor cannot simply reclaim them for personal use. The charitable remainder is legally committed to qualifying charitable purposes.

The structure should therefore be considered only when the donor genuinely intends to make the eventual charitable transfer.

## Costs, Administration, and Investment Risk

The tax advantages can make CRTs attractive, but the administrative requirements are significant.

A CRT must maintain appropriate records, calculate distributions correctly, report its financial activity, and generally file **Form 5227, Split-Interest Trust Information Return**, each year. Beneficiaries receive Schedule K-1 reporting their distributions.

There may also be legal, accounting, trustee, investment-management, and tax-preparation expenses.

Investment risk remains another consideration. A CRT does not guarantee income or investment performance. A CRUT's payments can fluctuate with the value of its assets, while a fixed CRAT payment can become less attractive if inflation rises or the portfolio performs poorly.

The trust also needs an investment strategy that balances current distributions with the long-term charitable remainder.

## 2026 Warning: Avoid Aggressive “Tax-Free” CRT Schemes

Investors should be especially cautious about arrangements marketed as a way to eliminate capital-gains or ordinary-income taxes entirely.

In July 2026, the IRS and Treasury finalized regulations identifying certain transactions involving purported CRATs as **listed transactions**. The arrangements targeted by the rules involve strategies that purportedly eliminate ordinary income or capital gains through the use of a CRAT and an annuity structure.

This development reinforces an important principle: a legitimate CRT is a highly regulated charitable-planning vehicle, not a mechanism for making taxable income disappear.

Anyone considering a CRT should have an estate-planning attorney and qualified tax professional review the proposed structure before transferring assets.

## When a CRT May Fit a Financial Plan

A CRT can be worth investigating when several circumstances come together:

* You own substantially appreciated assets.
* You want to diversify without immediately recognizing the entire gain personally.
* You need an income stream from the contributed assets.
* You have a genuine long-term charitable objective.
* You can permanently give up the transferred principal.
* Your estate and tax situation justify the legal and administrative costs.

It may be less appropriate when the donor needs unrestricted access to the assets, does not have a meaningful charitable objective, or is primarily seeking a way to eliminate taxes.

The most important question is not simply how much tax a CRT can defer. It is whether the combination of **income, charitable giving, investment management, and tax treatment** fits the donor's broader financial plan.

For investors with highly appreciated assets and philanthropic goals, a charitable remainder trust can turn an otherwise difficult planning problem into a coordinated strategy. But its advantages come from carefully balancing the donor's retained income interest with an irrevocable commitment to charity—not from making taxes disappear.
