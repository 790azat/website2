Investors with substantial assets often look beyond publicly traded stocks and bonds for additional ways to deploy capital. Private equity and venture capital are two areas of the private markets that can provide exposure to privately held businesses, but they operate very differently from traditional brokerage investments.

Access is also more restricted. Many private offerings are available only to **accredited investors**, and some private funds impose additional eligibility requirements. The regulatory framework is designed differently from the one governing publicly registered investments, which means investors may receive less standardized disclosure and face greater liquidity and valuation risks.

For investors considering these strategies, understanding the structure, eligibility requirements, fees, time horizon, and risks is essential before committing capital.

## What Is Private Equity?

Private equity generally involves investing in companies that are not publicly traded.

A private equity fund pools capital from investors and uses that capital to acquire or invest in businesses. Depending on the fund's strategy, it may purchase established companies, provide growth capital, restructure businesses, or acquire controlling interests.

The fund is generally managed by a private equity firm, while investors participate as limited partners.

Unlike buying shares of a publicly traded company, an investor in a private equity fund typically does not decide which individual companies the fund purchases. The fund manager makes investment decisions according to the fund's strategy and governing documents.

The SEC notes that private equity funds generally have long investment horizons and can be illiquid for several years.

## What Is Venture Capital?

Venture capital focuses primarily on privately held companies with significant growth potential, often at earlier stages of development.

A venture capital fund may invest in startups at stages such as:

* Seed
* Early stage
* Series A
* Series B
* Later-stage growth

The companies may operate in technology, healthcare, financial services, consumer products, biotechnology, or other industries.

Venture capital investing involves substantial uncertainty. Many early-stage businesses do not reach large-scale commercial success, while a smaller number may experience significant growth.

Because of this distribution of outcomes, venture capital portfolios can behave very differently from portfolios of established public companies.

## Private Equity vs. Venture Capital

Although both belong to the private markets, their investment approaches differ.

| Feature | Private Equity | Venture Capital |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| Typical company stage | Established businesses | Early-stage and growth companies |
| Investment approach | Acquisitions, buyouts, growth capital | Startup and growth financing |
| Ownership | Often substantial or controlling | Usually minority stakes |
| Business maturity | More established operations | Often developing business models |
| Investment horizon | Often several years | Often several years |
| Liquidity | Limited | Limited |
| Primary risks | Leverage, business performance, valuation, illiquidity | Business failure, dilution, valuation, illiquidity |

There can be overlap between the two categories. Some funds invest in later-stage startups, growth companies, or businesses that sit between conventional venture capital and traditional buyout strategies.

The fund's actual investment mandate is therefore more important than the label alone.

## Who Is an Accredited Investor?

The SEC's accredited-investor definition determines eligibility for many private securities offerings.

For individuals, one common qualification is having a **net worth exceeding \$1 million, excluding the value of the primary residence**, either individually or jointly with a spouse or partner. Another common qualification is having income exceeding **\$200,000 individually or \$300,000 jointly with a spouse or partner in each of the previous two years**, with a reasonable expectation of reaching the same income level during the current year.

The definition also includes additional categories based on professional credentials, investment knowledge, certain positions, and other criteria.

For example, individuals holding certain securities licenses can qualify under professional-knowledge provisions. Certain knowledgeable employees of private funds can also qualify for investments in the relevant private funds.

Accredited-investor status does **not** mean that an investor is guaranteed access to every private investment. Individual funds can establish their own eligibility requirements, minimum investments, and suitability standards.

## Accredited Investor vs. Qualified Purchaser

These terms are sometimes used interchangeably in casual financial discussions, but they are not the same regulatory concept.

An **accredited investor** can qualify for participation in certain private securities offerings under federal securities exemptions.

A **qualified purchaser** is a separate category associated with certain private funds, particularly funds relying on the Investment Company Act's Section 3(c)(7) exclusion.

As a result, an investor can meet the accredited-investor definition and still not qualify for a particular private fund.

This distinction becomes important when reviewing private-fund documents because the eligibility requirements should be checked for the specific offering rather than assumed from general wealth or income.

## How Investors Access Private Markets

There are several potential routes into private equity and venture capital.

### Direct Investments

An investor can sometimes invest directly in a private company.

This may provide more direct exposure to a particular business, but it also concentrates the investment in one company and can require extensive due diligence.

Private-company shares can also be difficult to sell because there may be no public exchange where the securities can be traded.

### Private Equity Funds

A private equity fund pools investor capital and spreads it across a portfolio of private companies.

This provides diversification within the private-equity strategy, although the investor remains exposed to the fund manager's investment decisions and the risks of the underlying businesses.

### Venture Capital Funds

Venture capital funds provide another pooled structure, generally focusing on startups and growth-stage companies.

Investors typically make a capital commitment to the fund rather than depositing the entire amount for immediate investment. The fund manager can then call capital as investments are made, subject to the fund's governing documents. The SEC describes venture capital funds as structures that typically accept capital commitments from investors.

### Private-Market Platforms

Some investment platforms provide access to private-company offerings or private funds.

The availability of these opportunities can vary considerably. Investors should determine exactly what security they are purchasing, who is issuing it, what fees apply, and what restrictions govern transfers or withdrawals.

## Understanding Capital Calls

Private equity and venture capital funds often use a commitment model.

Suppose an investor commits \$500,000 to a private fund. That does not necessarily mean the entire \$500,000 is invested immediately.

The fund may initially call \$100,000 and request additional capital over subsequent years as investment opportunities arise.

This creates a liquidity consideration that differs from buying a publicly traded ETF. An investor needs to have sufficient cash or liquid assets available to meet future capital calls.

Failing to meet a capital commitment can have contractual consequences, so investors should understand the fund's capital-call schedule and default provisions before committing.

## Why Private Investments Can Be Illiquid

Liquidity is one of the most important differences between private and public markets.

A publicly traded stock can generally be sold during market hours, subject to normal market conditions. A private equity fund may prevent investors from withdrawing capital for years.

The SEC specifically identifies illiquidity as a major characteristic of private equity investments and notes that investors may need to hold an investment for several years before realizing a return.

Venture capital can be even more difficult to exit because private startups may have no established secondary market.

An investor should therefore avoid committing money that could be needed for emergency expenses, near-term purchases, or other obligations.

## Fees and Expenses

Private-market investments can involve multiple layers of fees.

A traditional private equity or venture capital fund may charge:

* Management fees
* Performance-based compensation or carried interest
* Fund administration expenses
* Legal and accounting expenses
* Transaction-related expenses
* Other expenses allocated under the fund agreement

The exact structure varies by fund.

The SEC specifically advises private-equity investors to review offering documents and agreements for information about fees and expenses. It has also brought enforcement actions involving undisclosed or improperly allocated expenses.

Investors should therefore look beyond a headline management fee and calculate how all expenses can affect the net return.

## Valuation Can Be Less Transparent

Public companies have continuously observable market prices during trading hours.

Private companies do not.

A private equity or venture capital fund may value its portfolio companies periodically using valuation methodologies rather than a live market price. This can make the reported value of an investment less immediately observable than the value of a publicly traded security.

An increase in a fund's reported net asset value does not necessarily mean an investor could sell the position immediately at that exact price.

This distinction becomes particularly important when comparing private investments with publicly traded portfolios.

## Diversification Within an Alternative-Asset Portfolio

Private equity and venture capital can provide exposure to companies that may not be available through public markets.

However, adding a private-market investment does not automatically diversify an investor's overall financial position.

For example, an investor who already owns substantial technology stocks may inadvertently increase technology exposure by investing in a venture fund concentrated in software startups.

Likewise, a business owner may already have significant exposure to private-company risk through their own company. Adding another private-business investment could increase overall concentration rather than reduce it.

The relevant question is therefore how the private investment interacts with the investor's **entire balance sheet**.

## Due Diligence Before Investing

Private investments require careful review because standardized public-market information may be less extensive.

Investors can examine:

**Fund strategy:** What types of companies does the fund target?

**Manager experience:** What experience does the investment team have with the stated strategy?

**Portfolio construction:** How many investments are expected, and how concentrated can the fund become?

**Capital commitments:** When can the fund request additional capital?

**Investment period:** How long is the manager expected to make new investments?

**Fund term:** When is the fund expected to begin returning capital?

**Fees:** What management fees, carried interest, and fund expenses apply?

**Conflicts:** Does the manager operate other funds or affiliated businesses that could create conflicts?

**Reporting:** What information will investors receive and how frequently?

**Exit strategy:** How does the manager expect to realize investments?

The SEC recommends paying close attention to private-equity fund offering documents and the agreements governing the investment.

## Fraud and Private-Market Opportunities

Private investments can also attract fraudulent offerings.

Investors should be cautious of anyone claiming that an investment is "SEC approved" simply because a manager has filed documents with the SEC.

In August 2026, the SEC warned about scammers misusing **Exempt Reporting Adviser (ERA)** filings to create an appearance of legitimacy. The SEC emphasized that an ERA is not registered with the SEC and advises private funds rather than individual investors directly.

Investors should independently verify firms and professionals using SEC and FINRA resources rather than relying solely on promotional materials.

## The Role of Private Equity and Venture Capital in Wealth Planning

Private equity and venture capital can provide access to private companies and investment strategies that differ from traditional public-market portfolios.

At the same time, these investments can involve long holding periods, capital commitments, limited liquidity, complex fee structures, valuation uncertainty, and substantial risk of loss.

Accredited-investor status is primarily an eligibility standard; it should not be interpreted as evidence that a particular investment is suitable for every accredited investor.

For someone evaluating private-market exposure, the key considerations include the purpose of the allocation, available liquidity, total portfolio concentration, fund structure, manager experience, fees, capital-call obligations, and the specific risks of the underlying investments.

Private-market investing can therefore be considered within a broader wealth-management strategy, but the investment should be evaluated on its actual contractual terms rather than simply on the reputation of the asset class or manager.
