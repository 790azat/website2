For businesses with substantial accounts receivable, inventory, or other working-capital assets, traditional cash-flow lending is not always the only way to obtain financing. A company may have valuable assets on its balance sheet while experiencing uneven earnings, seasonal working-capital requirements, or limited access to conventional credit.

**Asset-based lending (ABL)** provides another financing structure by using eligible business assets as collateral. The most common collateral consists of **accounts receivable and inventory**. Instead of relying primarily on the company's projected cash flow or profitability, an ABL lender establishes a borrowing base tied to the value and quality of eligible collateral.

The Office of the Comptroller of the Currency (OCC) describes asset-based lending as a closely controlled form of collateral-based commercial lending in which lenders use borrowing-base formulas, advance rates, collateral monitoring, and field audits.

For companies managing significant working-capital needs, understanding how ABL works can help clarify whether this type of financing fits the business's liquidity strategy.

## What Is Asset-Based Lending?

Asset-based lending is commercial financing secured by specific business assets.

The most common collateral categories are:

* Accounts receivable
* Inventory
* Equipment
* Other eligible working-capital assets

In many ABL facilities, accounts receivable and inventory form the primary collateral pool.

The lender assigns an **advance rate** to eligible collateral and calculates how much the borrower can draw.

For example, if a lender accepts \$1 million of eligible accounts receivable and applies an 85% advance rate, the borrowing capacity attributable to those receivables would be approximately:

**\$1,000,000 × 85% \= \$850,000**

The actual borrowing base can be more complicated because lenders may exclude certain receivables, apply reserves, discount inventory values, or impose additional eligibility requirements.

The OCC notes that ABL facilities generally place greater emphasis on collateral than conventional cash-flow lending and are structured so collateral can be converted to cash if repayment problems arise.

## How an ABL Borrowing Base Works

The **borrowing base** determines how much credit is available at a particular point in time.

A simplified structure might look like:

**Eligible accounts receivable × advance rate**

**\+ Eligible inventory × advance rate**

**− Required reserves**

**\= Borrowing base**

Consider a company with:

* \$2 million of eligible accounts receivable
* 85% receivables advance rate
* \$1 million of eligible inventory
* 50% inventory advance rate
* \$100,000 in lender reserves

The simplified borrowing base would be:

\$2,000,000 × 85% \= \$1,700,000

\$1,000,000 × 50% \= \$500,000

\$1,700,000 \+ \$500,000 − \$100,000 \= **\$2.1 million**

The company could potentially borrow up to approximately \$2.1 million under this simplified structure, subject to the facility's other conditions.

The actual formula is determined by the loan agreement.

## Accounts Receivable as Collateral

Accounts receivable represent amounts customers owe a business for products or services already provided.

They can be attractive collateral because invoices can eventually convert into cash.

However, not every receivable necessarily has the same borrowing value.

An ABL lender may evaluate:

* Customer credit quality
* Invoice age
* Payment history
* Concentration among customers
* Disputed invoices
* Returns and credits
* Geographic exposure
* Related-party receivables
* Collection history

Older or disputed receivables may be excluded from the borrowing base.

For example, a lender might treat a recently issued invoice to a financially strong commercial customer differently from a 120-day-old invoice that is already subject to a payment dispute.

This is one reason the face value of accounts receivable does not necessarily equal the amount a lender will advance against them.

## Inventory as Collateral

Inventory can also support an ABL facility, although lenders typically apply more conservative advance rates than they do to high-quality receivables.

Inventory can be difficult to value and liquidate.

Its value may depend on:

* Product demand
* Condition
* Obsolescence
* Seasonality
* Location
* Shelf life
* Market prices
* Finished versus unfinished goods
* Specialized versus standardized products

A warehouse full of widely traded finished products may have a different liquidation profile from specialized components manufactured for a single customer.

The OCC's ABL guidance emphasizes collateral valuation and notes that inventory appraisals may use **net orderly liquidation value (NOLV)** rather than retail or historical cost as a relevant measure of collateral value.

## ABL vs. Traditional Cash-Flow Lending

The primary difference is what the lender emphasizes when assessing repayment.

| Feature | Asset-Based Lending | Cash-Flow Lending |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| Primary collateral | Receivables, inventory and other assets | May be secured or unsecured |
| Main underwriting focus | Collateral and borrowing base | Cash flow and debt-service capacity |
| Availability | Changes as collateral changes | Usually based more on committed loan amount |
| Monitoring | Often frequent | Generally less collateral-intensive |
| Field audits | Common in traditional ABL | Less central |
| Borrowing base | Central feature | Usually not central |
| Cash controls | May be extensive | Varies |
| Suitable use | Working capital and liquidity | Broad business financing |

These are general structural distinctions. Individual commercial facilities can combine elements of both approaches.

The OCC distinguishes fully monitored ABL from less tightly controlled forms of secured receivables and inventory financing.

## Why Companies Use ABL

ABL is often associated with **working-capital financing**.

A company may have to pay suppliers before its customers pay outstanding invoices. That creates a cash-flow gap.

For example:

1. A manufacturer purchases \$500,000 of materials.
2. It produces and ships products.
3. The customer receives 60-day payment terms.
4. The manufacturer must fund payroll, inventory, shipping, and operating costs while waiting for payment.

An ABL facility can provide liquidity against eligible receivables and inventory during this cycle.

The OCC identifies working capital as a major use of accounts receivable and inventory financing and also notes uses such as acquisitions, debt restructuring, and periods of financial stress.

## Seasonal Businesses and Revolving Credit

ABL can be particularly relevant when borrowing needs fluctuate.

A retailer, distributor, manufacturer, or wholesaler may need more financing before a busy season and less after inventory is sold.

Because borrowing capacity can be tied to eligible collateral, the available credit can increase as receivables or eligible inventory increase.

This creates a revolving relationship between the company's working-capital assets and its financing capacity.

However, this structure also means that a decline in eligible collateral can reduce borrowing availability.

A company cannot necessarily assume that a \$5 million credit facility will provide \$5 million of usable liquidity at all times.

The actual availability depends on the borrowing base.

## Advance Rates

The **advance rate** determines how much of an eligible asset's value the lender is willing to finance.

For example:

* Eligible receivables: 85% advance rate
* Eligible inventory: 50% advance rate

The different rates reflect differences in collateral quality and liquidation risk.

Receivables may have a relatively predictable conversion into cash when customers pay. Inventory can require additional time and expense to sell, and its value can deteriorate.

Advance rates are therefore not simply a measure of how much the asset is worth. They represent how much lending capacity the lender is willing to recognize against that asset.

The OCC specifically identifies prudent advance rates and borrowing-base controls as important elements of ABL risk management.

## Eligibility Requirements

An ABL lender generally establishes rules defining which assets qualify.

For accounts receivable, eligibility may depend on:

* Age
* Customer concentration
* Payment status
* Jurisdiction
* Contract terms
* Disputes
* Credit quality

For inventory, eligibility may depend on:

* Type
* Location
* Condition
* Valuation
* Obsolescence
* Marketability

The loan agreement can also establish reserves against particular risks.

For example, if a borrower has a large concentration of receivables from one customer, the lender may impose a concentration limit or reserve.

## Field Audits and Collateral Monitoring

ABL typically involves substantially more monitoring than a conventional business loan.

A **field audit** can involve reviewing:

* Customer invoices
* Accounts receivable aging
* Inventory records
* Purchase documentation
* Sales records
* Credit memos
* Internal controls
* Physical inventory
* Borrowing-base calculations

The purpose is to determine whether the collateral reported by the borrower actually exists, is properly valued, and meets the facility's eligibility requirements.

The OCC describes field audits as an integral part of ABL monitoring and notes that audits can help identify financial weakness, inaccurate records, and potential fraud.

## Cash Dominion and Lockbox Arrangements

Some ABL structures give lenders significant control over cash collections.

A **lockbox** arrangement can direct customer payments into an account controlled by or accessible to the lender.

The cash may then be applied according to the facility's terms.

This structure helps the lender monitor collateral conversion and reduce the risk that receivables are collected but the proceeds are diverted elsewhere.

The degree of cash control depends on the facility and its contractual provisions.

More intensive cash controls are one of the features that distinguish traditional fully monitored ABL from less restrictive secured financing.

## ABL Costs and Fees

ABL can involve more administrative costs than simpler business financing because the lender is continuously monitoring collateral.

Potential costs include:

* Interest
* Commitment fees
* Unused-line fees
* Origination or arrangement fees
* Field-audit fees
* Appraisal fees
* Legal fees
* Collateral monitoring costs
* UCC filing-related expenses
* Other administrative charges

The exact pricing depends on the lender, facility size, collateral quality, business risk, and overall structure.

Companies should compare the **total cost of available liquidity**, rather than looking only at the stated interest rate.

## ABL for Companies With Uneven Cash Flow

One potential reason companies consider ABL is that collateral availability can provide financing even when traditional cash-flow metrics are under pressure.

For example, a distributor may have:

* Significant receivables
* Valuable inventory
* Strong customer relationships
* Temporary earnings pressure
* A need for additional working capital

The collateral can provide a financing base that is different from a loan relying primarily on projected earnings.

That does not mean ABL is automatically available to financially distressed companies. Lenders still evaluate repayment prospects, collateral quality, management, controls, and other risks.

The OCC emphasizes that weak collateral controls or overly liberal advances can materially increase risk.

## Risks of Asset-Based Lending

ABL provides liquidity, but it also creates obligations that companies need to manage carefully.

### Borrowing-base reductions

If receivables become less eligible or inventory values decline, available credit can fall.

### Collateral concentration

Heavy reliance on a small number of customers can create additional risk.

### Monitoring requirements

Borrowers may need to provide frequent financial and collateral reporting.

### Cash-control restrictions

Lockboxes and cash-dominion arrangements can reduce management's flexibility over collections.

### Covenant and reporting requirements

The lender may require detailed compliance certificates and financial reporting.

### Cross-default and lien considerations

A company with several lenders must understand who has security interests in which assets.

The OCC has highlighted the risks that can arise when multiple lenders have competing claims over receivables or inventory, making clear collateral ownership and control particularly important.

## ABL vs. Factoring

Asset-based lending and factoring both use accounts receivable, but they are structurally different.

With **ABL**, the company generally borrows against eligible receivables and retains ownership of the receivables.

With **factoring**, a finance company purchases receivables, potentially with or without recourse depending on the arrangement.

The OCC treats factoring as a separate category within accounts receivable and inventory financing.

The choice can therefore involve more than comparing interest rates. Businesses should examine ownership, collection responsibilities, recourse, fees, customer notification, and the effect on financial statements.

## ABL vs. Business Lines of Credit

A conventional business line of credit can provide revolving access to funds, but its underwriting may rely more heavily on the company's financial statements, credit history, cash flow, and overall creditworthiness.

An ABL facility is more explicitly tied to a borrowing base.

That can make the available amount more dynamic.

For a company with \$3 million of eligible receivables, borrowing capacity may be substantially different from the same company after receivables decline to \$1.5 million.

This creates both an advantage and a risk: financing availability can grow with working-capital assets, but it can also contract when those assets deteriorate.

## ABL and Acquisitions

ABL facilities can sometimes be incorporated into acquisition financing.

A company acquiring another business may need liquidity for:

* Purchase consideration
* Inventory
* Payroll
* Supplier payments
* Integration costs
* Working capital

An ABL revolver can potentially form part of a larger financing package.

However, acquisition financing requires careful analysis of the acquired company's collateral, customer concentration, inventory, liabilities, and post-transaction borrowing base.

The OCC notes that receivables and inventory financing can be used in acquisition and restructuring situations.

## What Lenders Look For

An ABL lender is likely to focus heavily on the quality and control of collateral.

Important factors can include:

* Aging of receivables
* Customer payment history
* Customer concentration
* Inventory turnover
* Inventory valuation
* Internal accounting controls
* Management reporting
* Existing liens
* Legal ownership of collateral
* Historical borrowing-base performance
* Cash collections
* Business liquidity

Strong internal financial controls can therefore be particularly important.

A business may have significant assets but still encounter financing problems if its records cannot reliably establish the existence and value of those assets.

## A Simple ABL Example

Suppose a distributor has:

**Accounts receivable:** \$4 million
**Eligible receivables:** \$3.5 million
**Advance rate:** 85%

Receivables borrowing capacity:

**\$3.5 million × 85% \= \$2.975 million**

Now suppose the company has:

**Eligible inventory:** \$2 million
**Inventory advance rate:** 50%

Inventory borrowing capacity:

**\$2 million × 50% \= \$1 million**

Total borrowing base before reserves:

**\$2.975 million \+ \$1 million \= \$3.975 million**

If the lender requires \$175,000 in reserves, available borrowing capacity becomes approximately:

**\$3.8 million**

If the company already owes \$2.5 million under the facility, approximately \$1.3 million of additional availability would remain under this simplified example.

Actual borrowing-base calculations can be considerably more complex.

## Questions to Ask an ABL Lender

Before entering an ABL facility, a company should understand:

1. Which receivables qualify?
2. Which inventory qualifies?
3. What advance rates apply?
4. How are reserves calculated?
5. How frequently is the borrowing base reported?
6. How often are field audits conducted?
7. Who pays for audits and appraisals?
8. Is a lockbox required?
9. What cash-dominion provisions apply?
10. What financial reporting is required?
11. Are there customer concentration limits?
12. What happens if collateral values decline?
13. Are there minimum liquidity requirements?
14. What fees apply in addition to interest?
15. Are there minimum borrowing or unused-line fees?
16. Which other lenders have liens on company assets?
17. What happens after a borrowing-base deficiency?
18. Can the facility support acquisitions or other strategic uses?

These questions can reveal the practical cost and operating restrictions of the facility.

## Managing ABL Responsibly

Asset-based lending works by connecting financing availability to the value and quality of business assets.

That structure can be useful for companies whose working-capital requirements fluctuate or whose balance sheets contain substantial receivables and inventory.

But the same mechanism creates a key risk: **borrowing capacity can shrink when collateral deteriorates**.

A company that aggressively borrows against receivables and inventory may find itself with less available liquidity precisely when customers delay payments, inventory becomes obsolete, or sales decline.

Effective ABL management therefore requires more than tracking the outstanding loan balance. Management should regularly monitor receivable aging, customer concentrations, inventory turnover, borrowing-base availability, collateral values, cash collections, and upcoming obligations.

For companies with substantial working-capital assets, ABL can provide a flexible source of revolving liquidity. The financing is strongest when the collateral is clearly identifiable, accurately reported, readily convertible to cash, and supported by disciplined internal controls.

Ultimately, the key question is not simply how much a business owns. It is **how much of those assets a lender will recognize as eligible collateral, at what advance rate, and under what monitoring and cash-control requirements**.
